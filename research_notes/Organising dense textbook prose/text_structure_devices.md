# Text-structure devices in expository/technical writing: lists, tables, graphic organisers, paragraph/sentence design

Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[EMP]** = empirical finding (experiment, RCT, meta-analysis).
- **[EXP]** = expert opinion / argument / case analysis (e.g. Tufte, Gopen & Swan, CAIB).
- **[CONV]** = style-guide convention (Microsoft, Google, GOV.UK, etc.). These are house rules, not evidence.

DOI policy: a DOI is given only where it was seen on a publisher page or printed in the publisher's PDF. Where a DOI came only from a search summary or a third-party reference list, it is marked "(unverified)" or left out.

## Lists vs prose: comprehension, recall and the fragmentation critique

### Takeaway
There is little controlled research on bullets vs prose. What exists [EMP] says bulleting a set of **parallel, homogeneous** items improves recall of *those items*. The benefit shrinks when the items are heterogeneous. Bullets can also *reduce* recall of the surrounding text (Jansen 2014). Expert critique [EXP], led by Tufte and the Columbia Accident Investigation Board, is strong and well documented: bullet outlines drop the connectives (because, therefore, unless, only if) that carry causal and conditional reasoning, and they let a qualifier sink to a low-status sub-bullet. **Rule of thumb: bullet things that are parallel. Keep reasoning in sentences.**

### Cited Findings

**Empirical: bullets vs in-paragraph enumeration**
- [EMP] Frank Jansen (2014), "How bulleted lists and enumerations in formatted paragraphs affect recall and evaluation of functional text", *Information Design Journal* 21(2), 146–162. Five studies on functional texts (health flyers, direct-mail letters, recipes) compared a bulleted list with the same items written as an enumeration inside a paragraph. Findings:
  - In 3 studies, the bulleted format improved recall of the *list items*.
  - The benefit is mitigated when the items are heterogeneous.
  - In 2 studies, bullets *reduced* recall of content in the surrounding text.
  - In 2 studies, bullets improved readers' evaluation of the text.
  - Sources: [Benjamins catalogue](https://benjamins.com/catalog/idj.21.2.06jan); [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/290470586_How_bulleted_lists_and_enumerations_in_formatted_paragraphs_affect_recall_and_evaluation_of_functional_text). The DOI 10.1075/idj.21.2.06jan is inferred from the publisher URL [jbe-platform](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/idj.21.2.06jan), which returned 403, so the full text was not read.
- [EMP, weak] A blog-hosted MTurk experiment (n≈400) reported that bullets increased recall of three grocery items by 33% compared with a paragraph. It is not peer reviewed, so weight it low — [Lyngo Lab](https://www.lyngolab.com/bulleted-lists-memory.html).
- [EMP, web usability] Morkes & Nielsen (1997) tested a 7-page "Travel Nebraska" site with 51 experienced web users. "Scannable" text (bulleted lists, bolded keywords, shorter sections, more headings, captions) scored 47% better on a composite usability measure than promotional control text. Concise text scored 58% better, objective text 27%, and all three combined 124%. The composite was a geometric mean of task time, errors, memory, sitemap drawing and satisfaction. The authors acknowledge many individual comparisons were non-significant, and the combined score is driven heavily by the task-error measure. The study used one small site and fact-finding tasks, not learning of causal content — [NN/g, Morkes & Nielsen 1997](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/). Nielsen's companion Alertbox reported that 79% of test users scanned new pages and 16% read word by word — [Alertbox Oct 1997 mirror](https://paginaspersonales.deusto.es/abaitua/konzeptu/sgml/9710a.html).

**Empirical: enumeration and organisational signals (Lorch / Lemarié line)**
- [EMP] Lorch & Lorch (1996), "Effects of organizational signals on free recall of expository text", *Journal of Educational Psychology* 88(1), 38–48. Three experiments found:
  - Signals (headings, overviews, summaries) had **no effect when the text structure was simple**.
  - They did affect the amount and organisation of recall when the structure was complex.
  - When only half the topics were signalled, *unsignalled* content suffered. The authors read this as signals steering processing, not adding information — [UKY repository](https://scholars.uky.edu/es/publications/effects-of-organizational-signals-on-free-recall-of-expository-te/).
- [EMP/theory] Lemarié, Lorch, Eyrolle & Virbel (2008), "SARA: A text-based and reader-based theory of signaling". The search results did not confirm the venue; it is commonly cited as *Educational Psychologist* 43(1). The theory classifies signals (including enumeration and list formatting) by scope, realisation, location and information function. It predicts that a signal's effect depends on how *relevant* the signalled information is to the reader's goals and prior knowledge, and how *accessible* it is — [UKY](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/sara-a-text-based-and-reader-based-theory-of-signaling/). A follow-up test on headings: Lorch, Lemarié & Grant (2011), *Discourse Processes* 48(3) (DOI 10.1080/0163853X.2010.503526 per search summary, unverified) — [HAL](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-01876482v1).
- [EMP, meta-analytic] Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018), "A meta-analysis of how signaling affects learning with media", *Educational Research Review* 23, 1–24. Secondary summaries report 103 studies, N=12,201, retention g≈0.53 and transfer g≈0.33. **These effect sizes were not confirmed against the original** — [QIC-WD umbrella summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Signaling%20in%20Training%20111021.pdf); [1cademy listing](https://1cademy.com/node/a-meta-analysis-of-how-signaling-affects-learning-with-media/jhLu2vaVT6WD5leSHwf9).

**Expert critique: bullets fragment reasoning (Tufte, CAIB)**
- [EXP] Tufte, *The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint* (Graphics Press, Cheshire CT). 1st edition 2003, 27 pp. ([MUNI record](https://is.muni.cz/publication/509518?lang=en)); 2nd edition 2006, "Pitching Out Corrupts Within" (PDF copyright page). Sources disagree on the ISBNs — [library record](https://library.usi.edu/record/274473). The claims below are from the 2nd-edition PDF text — [PDF](https://www.literature.wolkersdorfer.info/literature/The%20Cognitive%20Style%20of%20PowerPoint%20Pitching%20Out%20Corrupts%20Within.pdf):
  - **The Boeing debris slide.** Six levels of hierarchy arrange 11 phrases. The reassuring title is undermined by a 4th-level bullet at the bottom: the debris was roughly 640× larger than the test data (1920/3 in³). Tufte argues the correct headline would have said the test data were irrelevant. "Significant/significantly" appears 5 times with incompatible meanings.
  - **Shorthand.** Bullet formats force acronyms, phrase fragments and vague pronouns ("it"), because clarifying phrases are "too long to fit".
  - **Anti-narrative.** Rigid slide-by-slide hierarchies "slice and dice the evidence", producing an "anti-narrative with choppy continuity". Every bullet's status is signalled 4–5 ways at once (order, indent, bullet size, bullet style, type size).
  - **Engineers' emails.** Of the several hundred NASA engineers' emails about the threat, about 90% used sentences in paragraphs and 10% used 2–3-level bullet lists. Tufte presents this as engineers reasoning better without deep outlines.
  - **Causal content.** "As analysis becomes more causal, multivariate, comparative, evidence-based … the more damaging the bullet list becomes." His recommendation is written reports and handouts built on sentences and paragraphs.
  - Tufte himself allows that lists suit quick, simple messages.
- [EXP, official] Columbia Accident Investigation Board, *Report* Vol. 1 (2003). Key statements:
  - p. 191: "The Board views the endemic use of PowerPoint briefing slides instead of technical papers as an illustration of the problematic methods of technical communication at NASA."
  - p. 191, same passage: a senior manager could read the slide "and not realize that it addresses a life-threatening situation".
  - Also cited: "The choice of headings, arrangement of information, and size of bullets on the key chart served to highlight what management already believed."
  - Sources: quoted in [Tufte's notebook](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/columbia-accident-investigation-board-the-boeing-powerpoint-slide/) and the Tufte PDF; report index at [UNT govinfo](https://govinfo.library.unt.edu/caib/news/report/volume1/default.html). Page numbers come via Tufte and other secondary sources; the report PDF itself was not opened.
  - The 2005 Return to Flight Task Group said PowerPoint should "never be allowed to replace, or even supplement, formal documentation" (quoted in the Tufte PDF).
- [EXP] Shaw, Brown & Bromiley (1998), "Strategic stories: How 3M is rewriting business planning", *Harvard Business Review* 76(3), May–June. Argues bullet-list plans list "good things to do" without specifying critical relationships, and that narrative fills the spaces around the bullets. Tufte quotes it as showing bullets leave "critical assumptions about how the business works unstated" — [Bookshare record](https://www.bookshare.org/browse/book/1331749); [Tufte PDF](https://www.literature.wolkersdorfer.info/literature/The%20Cognitive%20Style%20of%20PowerPoint%20Pitching%20Out%20Corrupts%20Within.pdf).
- [EXP, counterpoint] Doumont (2005), "The cognitive style of PowerPoint: Slides are not all evil", *Technical Communication* 52(1), 64–70. Argues the problems are in design and use, not inherent to slides. The full text was not read — [BibSonomy](https://www.bibsonomy.org/bibtex/7b68fcf9924d2c1eccc8eea12b70b46c).

**Style-guide conventions on lists**
- [CONV] Microsoft Writing Style Guide, "Lists" ([source](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/lists)):
  - Use at least 2 items and, if possible, no more than 7.
  - Keep items short enough that 2–3 are visible at a glance; a couple of short paragraphs per item is OK occasionally.
  - Keep items consistent in structure (parallel).
  - Bullets are for items with something in common but no required order. Numbers are for sequential or prioritised items.
  - Term lists (bold term + period + definition) are offered as "a common alternative to a table".
  - Introduce every list with a heading, a sentence, or a fragment ending in a colon.
  - No end punctuation unless the items are sentences.
- [CONV] Google developer documentation style guide, "Lists" ([source](https://developers.google.com/style/lists)):
  - Numbered lists are for sequence; bulleted lists for non-sequential items; description lists for term + explanation (or bullets with bold run-in headings).
  - **Use a table for structured data with multiple properties per item.** Use a list for sequences or simple collections.
  - Never use a list for a single item. "Use a list only when it adds clarity."
  - Usually introduce a list with a complete sentence. Keep items parallel. Avoid ending with "etc.".
- [CONV] APA, Chicago and GOV.UK list pages could not be retrieved (APA returned an empty page; GOV.UK pages redirected or returned 404; Chicago is paywalled). See Gaps.

### Inferences

**Reading the list evidence**
- The empirical benefit of bullets is narrow: better recall of the *bulleted items themselves*, when they are parallel and homogeneous. Jansen's surrounding-text cost and the "heterogeneous items" caveat directly support the claim that bulleting can fragment content. A bulleted block draws attention away from the explanatory prose around it.
- The strongest anti-bullet evidence (CAIB/Tufte) is case-based, not experimental. It is very persuasive for **causal, conditional, quantitative-caveat** content: exactly the "because / unless / only if / X times larger than" reasoning in pathophysiology and nutrition-evidence text.
- Morkes & Nielsen and Nielsen's scanning data apply to lookup and navigation on web pages. They do not show that bulleting improves understanding of mechanisms. A web textbook serves both uses: scanning on re-visit, deep reading on first pass.

**Cross-cutting decision rubric (synthesis across all sections of these notes)**
For a dense chunk, ask what the *logical relation between the units* is:
- **Causal chain or argument** (A because B, therefore C; unless D) → **prose**, with explicit connectives, topic sentences and given-new ordering. If the chain has 4 or more steps and readers need to retrace it, add a **flow diagram** as well, not instead.
- **Strict sequence** (steps, stages, a counselling protocol) → **numbered list**, or a flow diagram if there are branches.
- **Unordered set of parallel, homogeneous items** (sources of a nutrient; red-flag symptoms) → **bulleted list**, 2–7 short items, grammatically parallel, introduced by a sentence that says what unites them and whether the list is exhaustive.
- **Several items × several shared attributes** (vitamins × function/sources/deficiency/toxicity) → **table / matrix**, with rows and columns in a natural order (see the Tables section).
- **Hierarchy or classification** → a short nested list (2 levels at most), or a **tree diagram**.
- **Many-to-many relations / network** (a nutrient affecting several organ systems) → **concept map**, with labelled links. Pair it with prose for low-knowledge readers.
- **Continuum** (e.g. spectrum of malnutrition) → **spectrum / number-line figure**.
- **Mix:** explanatory paragraph → table or figure for the comparison → a sentence drawing the take-home relation out of the table. The prose carries the *why*; the device carries the *what/which*.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed experiment was found that compares bullets vs paragraphs for *causal/explanatory* science content with comprehension or transfer measures. Jansen covers functional texts. This is the key missing evidence.
- Chicago Manual of Style (§6.123–6.130, lists), APA 7 (lettered/numbered/bulleted lists; APA's own note that numbered lists can imply ranking) and GOV.UK bullet guidance could not be retrieved here. Treat any specific rule attributed to them as unverified.
- Exact effect sizes for Schneider et al. (2018) were not checked against the paper.
- The venue of the SARA paper was not confirmed in the search results.

## Tables: when tables beat text, and design rules

### Takeaway
For comparison and lookup across items that share attributes, the evidence favours tables or matrices over running text [EMP]:
- Kiewra's matrix studies show better relational learning than text or outlines.
- A large preregistered RCT found health "fact box" tables improved comprehension (d≈0.4), though recall without the materials improved only slightly.
- Design matters: a matrix only helps if its rows and columns are in a **natural, meaningful order** (Kauffman & Kiewra 2010).

### Cited Findings
- [EMP] Kauffman & Kiewra (2010), "What makes a matrix so effective? An empirical test of the relative benefits of signaling, extraction, and localization", *Instructional Science* 38, 679–705, DOI 10.1007/s11251-009-9095-8 (printed on the publisher PDF):
  - **Exp. 1** (n=72; 4×2 design: text, extracted key ideas, outline, matrix; each with or without signalling). The matrix was best for both fact and relationship learning, "because of its ability to localize related information within topics and categories". **"Simply signaling or extracting text ideas was not helpful."**
  - **Exp. 2** (n≈54). "Not all matrices are created equal": a "high, natural ordering of matrix topics is necessary to highlight relationships".
  - The authors' review says earlier studies found matrices superior to outlines, "particularly for relationship learning". They also note many earlier display studies used short, often fictitious texts; theirs used a 2,000-word factual text.
  - Source: [author PDF](https://kiewra.unl.edu/sites/unl.edu.cehs.kiewra/files/media/file/What%20Makes%20a%20Matrix%20So%20Effective.pdf).
- [EMP] Kiewra, Kauffman, Robinson, Dubois & Staley (1999), "Supplementing floundering text with adjunct displays", *Instructional Science* 27, 373–401. Reference details are as printed in Kauffman & Kiewra (2010). Per a search summary of the article ([doi.org link](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1003270723360), paywalled): outline and matrix displays produced more relational learning than text, and matrices more than outlines. The two-dimensional matrix "positioned related information … in closer proximity".
- [EMP] Brick, McDowell & Freeman (2020), "Risk communication in tables versus text: a registered report randomized trial on 'fact boxes'", *Royal Society Open Science* 7(3), 190876, DOI 10.1098/rsos.190876 ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7137953/)):
  - Sample: N=2,305 census-matched UK adults. Topics: antibiotics for ear infection and flu vaccine. Comparison: tabular fact box vs identical text.
  - Comprehension: 79.6% vs 69.7% correct, d=0.39 (95% CI 0.31–0.47); 77.5% vs 65.4% at follow-up, d=0.43.
  - Recall without materials at about 6 weeks: 29.8% vs 27.7%, d=0.12 ("slight").
  - Fact boxes were rated more engaging. There was no difference in trust or decisions.
  - The benefit held across numeracy levels and was possibly largest for low-numeracy readers (exploratory).
  - The authors judge the prior evidence for tables "limited" and note likely publication bias.
- [EMP, preference only] Kozak & Hartley (2012) on "text tables" (small tables embedded in a sentence). When the data suited the layout, readers strongly preferred text tables to the original sentences. This was a preference survey, not a comprehension test — [IDEAS/RePEc, JASIST 63(1) 108–113](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jamist/v63y2012i1p108-113.html).
- [EMP/theory] Larkin & Simon (1987), "Why a diagram is (sometimes) worth ten thousand words", *Cognitive Science* 11, 65–99. Informationally equivalent representations can differ in **computational** efficiency. Diagrams (and, by extension, spatial tables) index information by location, which reduces search and makes explicit relations that sentential text leaves to be computed — [full text PDF](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/F12/cs200/Readings/larkin.whyadiagramissometimesworth.1987.pdf). The DOI 10.1016/S0364-0213(87)80026-5 appears in Kauffman & Kiewra's reference list (unverified on Crossref).
- [EXP] Patricia Wright (MRC Applied Psychology Unit, later Cardiff) is the classic source on tables and on consulting information. Her 1998 paper argues against a single "best practice" for graphics in documentation, in favour of task-specific trade-offs — [Utah jcd.bib entry, Wright 1998](https://ftp.math.utah.edu/pub/tex/bib/idx/jcd/22/4/3_10.html); [Cardiff ORCA profile](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/32316). Her specific table-vs-prose lookup findings were not retrieved.
- [EXP] Hartley, *Designing Instructional Text* (Routledge; several editions) has a chapter on tables and graphs — [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Designing-Instructional-Text/HartleyJames/p/book/9780749410377). The chapter's specific claims were not retrieved.
- [CONV] Google style: use a table when items have **multiple properties**; a list for sequences or simple collections — [Google](https://developers.google.com/style/lists). Microsoft: term lists are an alternative to a 2-column table — [Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/lists).

### Inferences
- Use a table when the content is genuinely **items × shared attributes** and the reader's job is to compare across rows or look one up. Order rows and columns meaningfully: by mechanism, by severity, or by a shared category. Alphabetical order is weak unless the table is for lookup. Kauffman & Kiewra's Exp. 2 shows a badly ordered matrix loses much of the advantage.
- Tables seem to help *understanding while the table is in view* more than long-term memory (Brick et al.: d≈0.4 vs 0.12). The prose should still state the one or two relations the table exists to show.
- Two-column "term | definition" tables are often better as term lists (bold term + sentence). Keep tables for 3 or more attributes.
- A table cannot carry causal explanation. Kauffman & Kiewra found that extraction alone (pulling key ideas out of the text) did not help. The benefit comes from localising related facts, not from merely shortening the text.

### Gaps
- No primary source was retrieved for Wright's or Hartley's empirical table-vs-text work, or for Stephen Few's table-vs-graph rules (*Show Me the Numbers*). These are expert/practitioner sources in any case.
- Robinson & Kiewra (1995, *J. Educ. Psych.* 87(3) 455–467) is confirmed bibliographically, but its effect sizes were not read. Robinson & Schraw (1994, *Contemporary Educational Psychology*) is printed as "91, 399–415" in Kauffman & Kiewra's reference list; the volume is probably 19, so verify before citing. Robinson & Schraw raised the possibility that graphic organisers communicate relations "too effectively", reducing active processing (from its title; contents not read).

## Graphic organisers and concept maps (trees, flowcharts, matrices vs outlines)

### Takeaway
The meta-analytic evidence [EMP] is that node-link concept maps are associated with better retention than text, lists or outlines:
- The effect is larger when learners *construct* maps than when they *study* given ones. Studying a map vs studying text/lists/outlines gives a small-to-moderate effect.
- Effects are heterogeneous and depend on how maps are used and what they are compared with.
- Graphic organisers vs outlines: organisers (especially matrices) win for **relational** learning.

### Cited Findings
- [EMP, meta-analysis] Nesbit & Adesope (2006), "Learning with concept and knowledge maps: A meta-analysis", *Review of Educational Research* 76(3), 413–448:
  - 55 studies, 67 effect sizes, N=5,818, Grade 4 to postsecondary, including science, psychology, statistics and nursing.
  - Map use was associated with increased knowledge retention across many conditions. Effects ranged from small to large depending on use and comparison, with significant heterogeneity in most subsets.
  - Sources: [SFU author page](https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/research/nesbitadesope2006.htm). The DOI 10.3102/00346543076003413 came from a search summary (unverified).
  - Subgroup figures from a search summary of a later review, **not verified against the full text**: constructing maps g≈0.82; studying maps g≈0.37; studying maps vs outlines/lists g≈0.28 (k=10) — [search-summary sources incl. WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Learning-With-Concept-and-Knowledge-Maps/99900601055701842).
- [EMP, meta-analysis] Dexter & Hughes (2011), "Graphic organizers and students with learning disabilities: A meta-analysis", *Learning Disability Quarterly* 34(1), 51–72. 16 articles, 55 effect sizes, 808 students in Grades 4–12. Graphic organisers improved vocabulary, comprehension and inferential knowledge, with moderate-to-large effects varying by measure, organiser type and subject — [Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/graphic-organizers-and-students-with-learning-disabilities-a-meta/); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/073194871103400104). Note: these are school-age learners with learning disabilities, so transfer to medical students is indirect.
- [EMP] Robinson & Kiewra (1995), "Visual argument: Graphic organizers are superior to outlines in improving learning from text", *Journal of Educational Psychology* 87(3), 455–467. Two experiments with longer texts and delayed tests. The abstract notes that earlier work used short, poorly organised texts and immediate factual tests, and that there had been no convincing evidence that organisers beat outlines — [Scinapse](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/1965693187); bibliographic details confirmed in [Kauffman & Kiewra 2010 reference list](https://kiewra.unl.edu/sites/unl.edu.cehs.kiewra/files/media/file/What%20Makes%20a%20Matrix%20So%20Effective.pdf).
- [EMP] Matrix vs outline vs text: Kauffman & Kiewra (2010) and Kiewra et al. (1999), above. Matrix > outline > text for relationship learning. Natural ordering is required.
- [EMP, meta-analysis, adjacent] Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016), "Signaling text-picture relations in multimedia learning: A comprehensive meta-analysis", *Educational Research Review* 17, 19–36. 27 studies, 45 comparisons, N=2,464, r=0.17 for comprehension/transfer. **The benefit was moderated by prior knowledge: it was strongest for low-prior-knowledge learners** — [Tübingen repository](https://publikationen.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/74617?show=full). The DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2015.12.003 came from a search summary (unverified).
- [EMP/theory] Larkin & Simon (1987), above: diagrams win when they make relations explicit and searchable by location.

### Inferences
- Concept maps help most when **learners build them**. As textbook-supplied figures, the evidence supports a modest gain over equivalent text or outlines. That argues for using maps for *genuinely relational* content (many-to-many links), and for including them as learner activities (e.g. "sketch the links between…") rather than as decoration.
- **Trees** suit hierarchy/classification. **Flowcharts** suit sequence plus branching (decision or referral pathways). **Matrices** suit comparison. **Concept maps** suit networks. Choosing by the logical relation (Larkin & Simon's computational efficiency) is the unifying principle.
- Low-prior-knowledge readers (first-year medical students meeting nutrition biochemistry) are the group most likely to benefit from explicit signalling of relations (Richter et al.). They are also the group most hurt by gaps in prose cohesion (next section). Diagrams and cohesive prose are complements, not substitutes.

### Gaps
- Nesbit & Adesope subgroup g-values were not verified from the full PDF ([PDF link from the SFU page](https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/research/nesbitadesope2006.htm)). Verify before quoting numbers.
- No study was found directly on concept maps in medical or nutrition *textbooks* (as opposed to learner-constructed maps).

## Paragraph length, sentence length, readability formulas and cohesion

### Takeaway
Readability formulas (Flesch etc.) count sentence and word length but ignore cohesion, layout, content and reader knowledge. Technical-communication experts reject them as design targets [EXP].
The stronger evidence [EMP] is about **cohesion**: low-knowledge readers understand more from *more* cohesive, explicit text, with the gaps filled and the relations stated. High-knowledge readers can learn more deeply from less cohesive text, because it forces inference (McNamara et al. 1996; Ozuru et al. 2009).
Direct evidence that shorter sentences per se improve adult comprehension is thin. Chopping sentences can *remove* the connectives that build cohesion.

### Cited Findings
- [EMP] McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996), "Are good texts always better? Interactions of text coherence, background knowledge, and levels of understanding in learning from text", *Cognition and Instruction* 14(1), 1–43 — bibliographic details confirmed at [ASU SoLET Lab publications](https://soletlab.asu.edu/cohesion-publications/). Findings as reported by secondary sources (full text not read):
  - Low-knowledge readers benefited from high-coherence text, mainly on text-base measures.
  - High-knowledge readers did better on situation-model and inference measures after reading the *low*-coherence text.
  - Authors' conclusion: a text that "requires gap-filling inferences is beneficial for learning, provided the learner has an appropriate knowledge background".
  - Sources: [BYU Editing Research summary](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing); [CMU teaching summit PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/summit/2016/Delahay_Anita1.pdf).
- [EMP] Related but distinct: McNamara & Kintsch (1996), "Learning from texts: Effects of prior knowledge and text coherence", *Discourse Processes* 22(3), 247–288 — [Unibo index](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/2804815). A search engine conflated this paper with the *Cognition and Instruction* one; they are two papers.
- [EMP] Ozuru, Dempsey & McNamara (2009), "Prior knowledge, reading skill, and text cohesion in the comprehension of science texts", *Learning and Instruction* 19(3), 228–242. High-cohesion biology text benefited low-knowledge readers. High-knowledge readers benefited only under certain conditions (the details were cut off in the source) — [ASU SoLET](https://soletlab.asu.edu/reading-comprehension-publications); DOI 10.1016/j.learninstruc.2008.04.003 per search summary (unverified).
- [EMP/tool] Graesser, McNamara, Louwerse & Cai (2004), "Coh-Metrix: Analysis of text on cohesion and language", *Behavior Research Methods, Instruments, & Computers* 36(2), 193–202. The tool computes referential cohesion, connectives, latent semantic overlap and similar measures that traditional formulas lack — [Memphis Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/17131). The DOI 10.3758/BF03195564 came from a search summary (unverified). Book-length treatment: McNamara, Graesser, McCarthy & Cai (2014), *Automated Evaluation of Text and Discourse with Coh-Metrix*, Cambridge UP.
- [EXP] Redish (2000), "Readability formulas have even more limitations than Klare discusses", *ACM Journal of Computer Documentation* 24(3), 132–137, DOI 10.1145/344599.344637 (DOI from a doi.org result in the search). Her points:
  - Formulas were built for children's school texts, not adult technical documents.
  - They ignore reader differences, content and layout, and overweight countable features.
  - Usability testing with real readers is the better method.
  - Sources: [Utah jcd.bib](https://ftp.math.utah.edu/pub/tex/bib/idx/jcd/24/3/132_137.html); [Effortmark summary](https://effortmark.co.uk/?p=3586). Schriver's companion commentary in the same issue also judged the formulas unreliable — [jcd.bib](https://ftp.math.utah.edu/pub/tex/bib/idx/jcd/24/3/138_140.html). Klare's reprinted overview is in the same issue — [jcd.bib](https://ftp.math.utah.edu/pub/tex/bib/idx/jcd/24/3/148_168.html).
- [EMP, weak] Sentence length: one small within-subjects study (n=21, screen-reader users) found the best comprehension and lowest workload at 16–20-word sentences. The original was not checked — [search summary](https://www.scite.ai/authors/bam-bahadur-kadayat-JDYWn). Matthews & Folivi (2023, *PLOS ONE*) show readers perceive sentence length quickly by counting lines, but did not test comprehension — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9955962/).
- [CONV] GOV.UK's guidance (commonly cited as "25 words max" and "reading age 9") could not be retrieved this session; the pages redirected or returned 404. Not verified.

### Inferences
- For first-year medical students who are low in prior knowledge of nutrition biochemistry, the evidence points toward **more explicit, more cohesive prose**: repeated key nouns rather than vague "this/it", explicit connectives, and bridging inferences stated. It does not point toward shorter or more fragmented prose. Breaking a causal paragraph into bullets *lowers* cohesion: it removes connectives and referential overlap, which hurts exactly these readers.
- "Dense, hard to read" is more likely a **cohesion and signposting problem** (missing topic sentences, unstated links, too many new terms per sentence) than a length problem. Use readability scores only as a rough flag, never as a target. Coh-Metrix-style indices (referential overlap, connectives) are closer to the real problem.
- Reasonable practice [EXP/CONV, not strong evidence]: vary sentence length, and split sentences that stack several new terms or clauses. A paragraph should carry one main idea, announced by its first sentence.

### Gaps
- No robust adult RCT isolating sentence length (with syntax held constant) on comprehension of expository science text was found. The often-repeated "American Press Institute" comprehension-by-sentence-length figures were not traced to a primary source and should not be cited.
- Paragraph-length research (e.g. on screen) was not found in this session.

## Topic sentences, given-new ordering and cohesion as alternatives to breaking text up

### Takeaway
Psycholinguistic evidence [EMP] shows readers integrate a sentence faster when its *given* information (already mentioned) comes first and links to a clear antecedent, and the *new* information comes after (Haviland & Clark 1974). Gopen & Swan (1990) turned this into writing advice [EXP]: put old information in the topic position and new or emphasised information in the stress position at the sentence's end. Together with topic sentences and signalling, these are ways to make dense prose easier to read without fragmenting it.

### Cited Findings
- [EMP] Haviland & Clark (1974), "What's new? Acquiring new information as a process in comprehension", *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior* 13, 512–521. The "given-new contract": readers split a sentence into given and new parts, search memory for a unique antecedent for the given part, then attach the new information. In reading-time experiments, sentences with a direct antecedent were processed faster than those that needed a bridging inference — [Stanford, Clark papers archive](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/); [Clark & Haviland 1977 chapter PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/Clark,%20H.H.%20_%20Haviland,%20S.E.%20_Comprehension%20and%20the%20given-new%20contract_%201977.pdf).
- [EXP] Gopen & Swan (1990), "The science of scientific writing", *American Scientist* 78(6), 550–558. Readers naturally emphasise material at the end of a sentence (the "stress position"). The start (the "topic position") should link back to what is already known. Misplacing old and new information is a common writing problem — [quote compilation, TU/e](https://www.win.tue.nl/~wstomv/quotes/science-of-scientific-writing.html); [AcaWiki summary](https://acawiki.org/The_Science_of_Scientific_Writing).
- [EMP] Signalling helps most when the structure is complex and the signal shows organisation (Lorch & Lorch 1996), and for low-knowledge readers (Richter et al. 2016). See the earlier sections.
- [EMP] Low-knowledge readers benefit from cohesive, explicit text (McNamara et al. 1996; Ozuru et al. 2009). See the previous section.
- [EXP] Tufte notes that Feynman's 1,800-page physics textbooks use no bullets and only 2 levels of hierarchy (chapters and subheads). He offers this as a model of explanation through sentences — [Tufte PDF](https://www.literature.wolkersdorfer.info/literature/The%20Cognitive%20Style%20of%20PowerPoint%20Pitching%20Out%20Corrupts%20Within.pdf).

### Inferences
- Before converting a dense paragraph to a list, try a **cohesion rewrite**:
  1. A topic sentence stating the claim.
  2. Each following sentence opening with given information (the previous sentence's key noun), with the new term at the end.
  3. Explicit connectives (because, so, however, only when).
  4. One new technical term per sentence where possible.
- Only if the content is then revealed to be a parallel set, a sequence or a comparison should it become a list, table or figure.
- A one-sentence "preview" naming the parts ("Three factors drive X: A, B and C") gives the enumeration benefit (Lorch/Lemarié signalling) while keeping the reasoning in prose.

### Gaps
- No experiment was found that tests Gopen & Swan's stress-position advice directly. It rests on given-new psycholinguistics plus expert judgement.
- Topic-sentence experiments (e.g. Kieras's work on initial thematic sentences) were not retrieved this session.

## Bold lead-ins, run-in headings and typographic emphasis (and overuse)

### Takeaway
Typographic cueing (underlining, capitals, bold) improves memory for the cued items by steering attention [EMP]. **Heavy cueing cancels the benefit**: when half the non-target sentences were also cued, recall fell back to the level of uncued text (Lorch, Lorch & Klusewitz 1995). Bold lead-ins and run-in headings are style-guide conventions for term lists [CONV]; there is no direct evidence on them.

### Cited Findings
- [EMP] Lorch, Lorch & Klusewitz (1995), "Effects of typographical cues on reading and recall of text", *Contemporary Educational Psychology* 20 — [UKY repository](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/effects-of-typographical-cues-on-reading-and-recall-of-text/):
  - **Exp. 1:** a four-page text with no underlining, light signalling (targets only) or heavy signalling (targets plus half the non-target sentences). **Cued recall was better under light signalling than in either the control or the heavy condition; control and heavy did not differ.**
  - **Exp. 2:** capitalised targets were read more slowly and remembered better.
  - The authors conclude that cues work through attention allocated during reading.
- [EMP] Morkes & Nielsen (1997): "scannable" web text, which included bolded keywords, improved composite usability by 47% for lookup tasks. Bolding was bundled with other changes, so its effect cannot be isolated — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/).
- [EMP, meta-analytic] Signalling in general aids retention more than transfer (Schneider et al. 2018, unverified figures), and helps low-knowledge learners most (Richter et al. 2016). See above.
- [CONV] Microsoft: bold the term in term lists (term. definition), "an exception to the general guideline of using italic for emphasis" — [Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/lists). Google: compact description lists use "bulleted items with bold run-in headings". Be consistent about punctuation, and don't use a dash between term and description — [Google](https://developers.google.com/style/lists).

### Inferences
- Bold works by **scarcity**. If many sentences per page carry bold lead-ins or bolded phrases, the cue stops discriminating (Lorch et al. 1995). Reserve bold for (a) the first defining use of a key term, and (b) run-in labels in a genuine term list. Don't bold whole clauses for emphasis.
- Bold run-in lead-ins at the start of every paragraph turn prose into a disguised list. They carry the same risk Jansen found for bullets: the labelled fragments get attention and the connecting prose loses it. Use them where paragraphs really are parallel entries (e.g. one per nutrient), not for a continuous argument.

### Gaps
- No experiment was found that isolates bold *run-in headings* vs plain topic sentences in expository text.
- NN/g's specific guidance on bolding was not retrieved; only secondary summaries surfaced.
- Schriver, *Dynamics in Document Design* (1997), is a key expert source on typographic emphasis and document design, but was not accessed this session.
