# Learning-science principles for structuring expository textbook prose

Scope: evidence from multimedia learning (Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning, CTML) and cognitive load theory (CLT) that bears on how to write and organise textbook prose for medical/nutrition students. Effect sizes are Hedges' g or Cohen's d unless stated. ~0.2 = small, ~0.5 = medium, ~0.8 = large (Cohen convention).

**Citation-verification key.** "DOI confirmed" = the DOI appeared on a publisher, doi.org, PubMed, or institutional-repository record that I retrieved in this session. "DOI per secondary source" = I saw the DOI only in a search summary or secondary page, so check it before publishing. Where I could not confirm an ID, I left it out. Many effect sizes below come from the abstract or from a faithful secondary summary rather than the paper's tables; those cases are flagged.

**Cross-cutting caveat for the report writer.** Most of this literature is short, lab-based experiments: 5–20 minutes of material, an immediate test, and university psychology students or school children. The rules are robust in direction, but the effect sizes are probably inflated for long, self-paced, multi-week textbook study. A 2025 meta-analysis of Mayer's own corpus found a pooled g = 0.37 that is declining year on year ([Cromley & Chen 2025, NSF PAR record](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)). That is smaller than the "median d" figures in Mayer's own tables.

## Signalling: headings, previews/overviews, topic sentences, typographic cues, connectives

### Takeaway
Signalling reliably helps. The broadest meta-analysis (Schneider et al. 2018) found g = 0.53 for retention and 0.33 for transfer, with text structure cues (headings, bold key words) at g = 0.71 for retention. University students benefit most. The benefit comes from steering attention, so it disappears when cues are overused (light underlining helped; heavy underlining equalled no cues) and when cues are distracting (blinking was harmful). Headings beat preview sentences for drawing attention to topic structure. Explicit causal connectives aid comprehension.

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses**
- **Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018), "A meta-analysis of how signaling affects learning with media", *Educational Research Review* 23, 1–24.** 103 studies, 12,201 participants, 1970–2016, mostly controlled lab studies. Retention g = 0.53 (139 comparisons) and transfer g = 0.33 (70 comparisons). Signalling also reduced cognitive load (g = 0.25), raised motivation slightly (g = 0.13), and increased learning time (g = 0.30). Eye-tracking showed signalled passages were fixated longer (g = 0.39). — [TUM Clearing House Unterricht review of Schneider et al. 2018 (German PDF, read in full)](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/CHU_KR-22_-Schneider_2017_Lernmaterial_Hervorhebungen.pdf). DOI not confirmed.
  - Moderators, from the same review's Table 1:
    - Age: university students g = 0.60 retention and 0.32 transfer (108/52 comparisons). Primary school children: n.s.
    - Prior knowledge: high-prior-knowledge learners benefited *more* (0.67 / 0.55) than low (0.42 / 0.39).
    - Signal type: text cues inside the text g = 0.68 / 0.48. "Structuring aids, e.g. headings or bold words" g = 0.71 retention, 0.35 transfer (57/9 comparisons). Colour g = 0.70 (k = 2, n.s.).
    - Distracting cues: blinking in pictures g = −0.56 / −0.47 (k = 3, n.s. but negative).
    - Duration: permanent cues (as in a textbook) g = 0.58 / 0.31.
    - Subject: biology g = 0.35 / 0.38. Mathematics ≈ 0 (0.08 / −0.03).
    - Caveat: the moderator cells are often small, so treat them with caution. — [same source](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/CHU_KR-22_-Schneider_2017_Lernmaterial_Hervorhebungen.pdf)
- **Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016), "Signaling text-picture relations in multimedia learning: A comprehensive meta-analysis", *Educational Research Review* 17, 19–36.** 27 studies, 45 comparisons, N = 2,464. Signalling text–picture correspondences gave r = 0.17 (95% CI .11–.22; ≈ d 0.35) for transfer and comprehension. The effect was moderated by prior knowledge: it was effective mainly for **low**-prior-knowledge learners. — [Tübingen repository / search record](https://publikationen.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/74617?show=full); data at [PsychArchives](https://psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.12034/2019). DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2015.12.003 per secondary source.
  - Note the conflict with Schneider et al. on prior knowledge. Richter et al. find low-knowledge learners benefit more; Schneider et al. find high-knowledge learners benefit more. Richter, Scheiter & Eitel also published a follow-up study on prior knowledge (*Journal of Educational Psychology* 2018, 110(4), 544–560), which is separate from the meta-analysis.
- **Alpizar, Adesope & Wong (2020), "A meta-analysis of signaling principle in multimedia learning environments", *Educational Technology Research and Development* 68(5), 2095–2119.** 29 studies, 44 effects, N = 2,726. Overall d = 0.38. Effects were larger in higher-quality studies, studies that reported outcome reliability, studies with a pretest, and studies that controlled for prior knowledge. — [WSU repository record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842). DOI confirmed on the repository record: 10.1007/s11423-020-09748-7.
- **Mayer corpus meta-analysis.** Across 92 articles and 591 effects, "cueing" (signalling) produced a medium significant effect. Removing seductive details and sentence-level coherence were among the largest. — [Cromley & Chen (2025), *Educational Research Review* 49, 100730, NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927). DOI confirmed on the record: 10.1016/j.edurev.2025.100730.

**Over-signalling**
- **Lorch, Lorch & Klusewitz (1995), "Effects of typographical cues on reading and recall of text".** College students read a 4-page text, in three versions:
  - no underlining;
  - targets underlined ("light signalling");
  - targets plus half the non-target sentences underlined ("heavy signalling").

  Cued recall was better in the light condition than in either the control or the heavy condition, and those two did not differ. Capitalising targets slowed reading and improved memory for them. The authors conclude that typographic cues work by redirecting attention during reading. Over-cueing therefore erases the advantage. — [University of Kentucky scholars record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/effects-of-typographical-cues-on-reading-and-recall-of-text/). Journal and DOI not confirmed in this session; usually cited as *Contemporary Educational Psychology*, so verify before citing.

**Headings vs previews; theory of signals**
- **Lorch, Lemarié & Chen (2013), "Signaling topic structure via headings or preview sentences", *Psicología Educativa* 19(2), 59–66.**
  - When reading for understanding, headings improved memory for subtopics. Headings and preview sentences did not differ for simple facts.
  - When outlining, any topic-structure signal helped compared with none, and headings and previews did not differ reliably.
  - Previews "do not elicit readers' attention to topic information as readily as headings do."

  — [UKY scholars record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/signaling-topic-structure-via-headings-or-preview-sentences/). DOI confirmed on the record: 10.1016/S1135-755X(13)70011-3.
- An earlier Lorch study found no signalling effect when the text's topic structure was simple. When the structure was complex, signals changed the distribution and organisation of recall. — reported via [search summary of Lorch work citing Lorch 1989](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/6137/613775409002/613775409002.pdf). This is a secondary report, so verify the specific study.
- **Lemarié, Lorch, Eyrolle & Virbel (2008), "SARA: A text-based and reader-based theory of signaling", *Educational Psychologist* 43, 27–48.** Signals are classified by information type, scope, realisation and location. Their effect depends on whether the signalled information is **relevant** to the reader's goals and prior knowledge, and **accessible** to the reader's cognitive processes. SARA = Signal, Available, Relevant, Accessible. — [UKY scholars record](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/sara-a-text-based-and-reader-based-theory-of-signaling/). DOI not confirmed.
- **Lorch (1989), "Text-signaling devices and their effects on reading and memory processes", *Educational Psychology Review*.** This is the standard review. It lists headings, preview statements, overviews and logical connectives as devices that direct attention to topics and organisation. — cited secondarily in [Psicología Educativa article (Redalyc PDF)](https://www.redalyc.org/journal/6137/613775409002/613775409002.pdf). Full text and DOI not retrieved.
- **Functional headings.** Headings that state a section's *purpose* (e.g. "Summary") steered selective attention under time pressure. Recall of summary content was M = 24.92 vs 13.98 with generic labels (ηp² = .09; N = 120 US college students). Headings that mismatch the content slowed reading of the following sentences. — [Chen, Thomas & McClure (2023), *Psicología Educativa* 29(2), 133–141](https://journals.copmadrid.org/psed/art/psed2023a14). DOI confirmed on publisher page: 10.5093/psed2023a14. These are single studies, not a meta-analysis.

**Connectives**
- **Degand & Sanders (2002), "The impact of relational markers on expository text comprehension in L1 and L2", *Reading and Writing* 15(7–8), 739–757.** Readers comprehended expository text better when causal relations were marked explicitly (connectives such as "because", "so", or signalling phrases). This held in both first and second language. Implicit versions differed significantly from explicit versions, and the explicit variants did not differ from each other. The earlier literature had reported facilitating, interfering or null effects. — [Utrecht University research portal](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/the-impact-of-relational-markers-on-expository-text-comprehension/). DOI not confirmed.

### Inferences
- For an authoring guide:
  - Use descriptive headings at every real topic shift. Headings outperform preview sentences at drawing attention to structure.
  - Bold only the few key terms or claims per section. The light-vs-heavy underlining result is the direct evidence against bolding many phrases.
  - Make causal and contrastive relations explicit with connectives ("because", "therefore", "in contrast"), especially for readers with English as a second language. This applies in Singapore.
  - Headings should name the content or function. Misleading or generic headings cost reading time.
- The Schneider et al. moderators suggest medical students are the population for whom text signalling works best: university-age, often with decent prior knowledge, reading permanent cues in text.
- Over-signalling hurts mainly by diluting the cue's ability to discriminate important from unimportant text. "Cue sparingly" is therefore more defensible than "cue everything important".

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analytic effect size specifically for **topic sentences** or for preview/overview paragraphs in plain prose. The evidence is from individual Lorch studies.
- Exact effect sizes from Lorch 1989 and Lorch et al. 1995 were not retrieved. The Lorch et al. 1995 journal/DOI and the Schneider 2018 DOI were not confirmed.
- The prior-knowledge moderator conflicts between meta-analyses (Richter vs Schneider) and is unresolved.

## Segmenting / chunking and working-memory limits (implications for paragraph length)

### Takeaway
Breaking a lesson into segments gives small-to-medium gains in retention and transfer and lowers cognitive load (Rey et al. 2019 meta-analysis). This evidence comes almost entirely from system-paced animations or videos, not static text. Working memory holds about 4 chunks (Cowan 2001). Neither line of evidence yields a specific evidence-based paragraph length.

### Cited Findings
- **Rey, Beege, Nebel, Wirzberger, Schmitt & Schneider (2019), "A meta-analysis of the segmenting effect", *Educational Psychology Review* 31, 389–419.**
  - 56 investigations, 88 pairwise comparisons.
  - Significant segmenting effect with small-to-medium effects on retention and transfer.
  - Segmentation reduced overall cognitive load and increased learning time.
  - Effects were confirmed for system-paced segmentation. Proposed mechanisms: chunking and structuring, extra processing time, and learner control of pace.
  - High-prior-knowledge learners gained more than low, for retention only.

  — [Max Planck Institute record](https://is.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting); [doi.org record](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4). DOI confirmed: 10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4. The exact g values are not in the abstract I saw.
- **Cowan (2001), "The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 24(1), 87–114.** Proposes a central capacity limit averaging about 4 chunks, in place of Miller's 7±2. The limit is observable only when rehearsal and recoding into larger chunks are prevented. — [PubMed 11515286](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/); [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/magical-number-4-in-shortterm-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496). DOI 10.1017/S0140525X01003922 is the PII in the Cambridge PDF URL. A published commentary warns of "the dangers of taking capacity limits too literally" (Avons, Ward & Russo), listed alongside the target article.
- Mayer's handbook groups segmenting with pre-training and modality as principles for *managing essential processing*, i.e. intrinsically complex material. — [Cambridge Handbook chapter listing](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/4110A2275F6DCD02DAB1F8B37BA7E5CE)

### Inferences
- The textbook analogue of segmenting is to split a complex causal chain into short, separately headed, learner-paced units. One mechanism step per paragraph, with a heading or signal between steps, is a reasonable reading of the evidence.
- Learners who can pause at the end of each unit (e.g. an inline check question) get the "extra processing time" mechanism.
- Cowan's ~4-chunk limit supports keeping the number of new, interacting elements a reader must hold at once small, e.g. not introducing more than a handful of new terms or quantities in one paragraph. It does not translate into a word count. Experts chunk more, so the same paragraph imposes less load on them.
- Static text is already learner-paced, so the segmenting benefit for text is probably smaller than in the animation studies. Its main value for text is structuring.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis or strong experiment establishing an optimal paragraph or section length for expository text. Any numeric rule (e.g. "≤150 words") would be a heuristic, not evidence-based.
- The segmenting effect sizes (g) were not retrieved from the paper.

## Coherence principle and the seductive-details effect

### Takeaway
Adding interesting but irrelevant material hurts learning. Meta-analytic estimates range from g ≈ −0.16 (a preliminary multilevel meta-analysis) through g = −0.33 (Sundararajan & Adesope 2020) to medium negative effects (Rey 2012). The harm is greater for novices, under time limits, and for static or paper materials. Removing seductive details is among the largest effects in Mayer's corpus. The effect is real but smaller and more variable than early estimates.

### Cited Findings
- **Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), "Keep it coherent: A meta-analysis of the seductive details effect", *Educational Psychology Review* 32(3), 707–734.**
  - About 58 studies and 68 effects; overall g = −0.33.
  - More detrimental for static details, paper materials, when backtracking was allowed, and with no prior knowledge.
  - Sources disagree on whether format and placement moderate the effect. The QIC-WD summary says format, timing, size, placement, frequency and inclusion of learning objectives did *not* moderate it. Another summary says details at the end and in text-plus-image format were worse.
  - Most studies used 6–12 minute sessions, and only cognitive outcomes were measured.

  — [e-teaching.org summary](https://www.e-teaching.org/materialien/literatur/sundararajan-adesope-2020); [WSU news release](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning); [QIC-WD umbrella summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Seductive%20Details%20in%20Training%20041821.pdf). DOI 10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4 per secondary source.
- **Rey (2012), "A review of research and a meta-analysis of the seductive detail effect", *Educational Research Review* 7(3), 216–237.**
  - 39 experimental effects. Small-to-medium negative effect on retention and medium on transfer.
  - Stronger with limited (vs unlimited) study time and when details were visual.
  - Secondary sources quote recall g = −0.27 to −0.95 and transfer g = −0.65 to −0.83.

  — [Learning Scientists guest post](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2019/6/20-1); [Eitel et al. PMC review "Inconsistent seduction"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8442593/). DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2012.05.003 per secondary source. The numbers are secondary and inconsistent across sources.
- **Contestation.** "Inconsistent seduction: Addressing confounds and methodological issues in the study of the seductive detail effect" (PMC) argues the literature has confounds: added text length or time, and the definition of "interesting". It is part of why the effect is described as inconsistent. — [PMC8442593](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8442593/). Content not read in detail.
- A later multilevel meta-analysis presented at AERA reported g = −0.16 across 177 effect sizes. This is a conference abstract and not peer-reviewed. — [AERA 2026 paper summary](https://convention2.allacademic.com/one/aera/aera26/online_program_direct_link/view_paper/2273554/)
- **Mayer's own tallies of the coherence principle:**
  - 2014 Cambridge Handbook: supported in 23/23 tests, median d = 0.86.
  - 2005 edition: 10/11 tests, median d = 1.32.
  - A 12-principles review lists coherence at d = 0.70.

  These are lab tests by Mayer's group, mostly transfer after short lessons. — [Cambridge Handbook extraneous-processing chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44); [Cambridge "Coherence principle" chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/multimedia-learning/coherence-principle/4E80B70CB76E2166B76E5653EBDE7D3E)
- In the Mayer-corpus meta-analysis, "removing seductive detail" and "sentence-level coherence" were among the largest significant effects. Exact g values are not given on the record page. — [Cromley & Chen 2025](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)
- **What counts as a seductive detail.** Interesting, emotionally engaging, or entertaining content (text, images, video) that is *related to the topic but irrelevant to the instructional objective*, e.g. anecdotes, trivia, decorative pictures. — [ACU Inspire summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details); [Learning and the Brain blog](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/seductive-details-when-do-cool-stories-and-videos-interfere-with-learning/)

### Inferences
- For the book, a "seductive detail" test asks whether a sentence, box, image or story serves the section's learning objective.
- Clinically relevant cases, worked patient vignettes, and Singapore-practice context that carries the point are **not** seductive details. Trivia, historical asides, decorative stock photos and "fun facts" that do not feed the objective are.
- Under the coherence principle, removing extraneous words also counts. "Sentence-level coherence" (tight, relevant sentences) is a lever for the "dense, hard to read" complaint, separate from shortening.
- Because novices are hurt most, first-year medical students reading a new domain are the at-risk group.
- Note a tension: long-form textbook reading is self-paced. Rey found the harm is stronger with time limits, so the penalty in a self-paced book may be closer to the smaller estimates. The effect is still negative, never positive, for learning outcomes.

### Gaps
- I did not resolve whether seductive details have any motivational benefit that offsets the cognitive cost in long courses. The meta-analyses measured only cognitive outcomes.
- The conflicting reports of Sundararajan & Adesope's format and placement moderators need checking against the paper.

## Redundancy principle and the expertise-reversal effect

### Takeaway
Presenting the same information twice in forms that must both be processed can hurt, especially for learners who already understand it. That covers a diagram that is self-explanatory plus prose restating it, or on-screen text duplicating narration with pictures. Instructional support that helps novices (integrated explanations, worked steps, fully explicit text) becomes redundant and can harm more knowledgeable learners: the expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga et al. 2003; Kalyuga 2007). For plain text without pictures, adding redundant written text to speech is neutral or helpful, so "redundancy" is not a blanket ban on repetition.

### Cited Findings
- **Adesope & Nesbit (2012), "Verbal redundancy in multimedia learning environments: A meta-analysis", *Journal of Educational Psychology* 104(1), 250–263.**
  - 57 independent studies, mostly postsecondary participants.
  - Spoken + written presentations did not differ from written-only, but beat spoken-only.
  - The advantage of verbal redundancy appeared for **low-prior-knowledge** learners, system-paced materials, and **picture-free** materials.

  — [WSU repository record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Verbal-Redundancy-in-Multimedia-Learning-Environments/99900601157101842). DOI 10.1037/a0026147 per secondary source. The pooled g was not retrieved.
- A PMC literature review, "Two types of redundancy in multimedia learning", distinguishes redundancy of *content* from redundancy of *modality*. It states that adding written text to spoken text usually supports learning. — [PMC10192876](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10192876/)
- **Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller (2003), "The expertise reversal effect", *Educational Psychologist* 38(1), 23–31.** The effectiveness of CLT-based techniques depends on learner expertise. Techniques that help novices can lose effectiveness or become harmful for more expert learners, because experts must process guidance that overlaps with their existing schemas. That overlap is redundant and adds working-memory load. Author order is listed variously, with Kalyuga first in most reference lists. — [University of Wollongong repository](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136); [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect). DOI not confirmed.
- **Kalyuga (2007), "Expertise reversal effect and its implications for learner-tailored instruction", *Educational Psychology Review* 19, 509–539.** Review. — [doi.org record](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3); DOI confirmed: 10.1007/s10648-007-9054-3.
- **McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996), "Are good texts always better? Interactions of text coherence, background knowledge, and levels of understanding in learning from text", *Cognition and Instruction* 14, 1–43.**
  - Text-level analogue of expertise reversal, in middle-school students. All students recalled more from the more coherent (fully explicit) text.
  - But **high-knowledge** readers did better on situation-model (deep understanding) tests with a *less* coherent text that forced gap-filling inferences.
  - Low-knowledge readers needed the explicit text.

  — [BYU editing research summary](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing); [UW PDF citing it](https://sites.uw.edu/ccdl/files//2013/12/Long_Assessing-text_2006.pdf). This is child/adolescent evidence, but the effect is widely replicated in the McNamara programme (not verified here).
- **Spatial contiguity × redundancy.** Integrating text and picture helps most when picture and text **complement** each other without duplicating content (g = 0.74, k = 28) or when the text is self-sufficient (g = 0.60, k = 25). It helps less when the picture is self-explanatory (g = 0.26, k = 4, n.s.). — [TUM Clearing House review of Schroeder & Cenkci 2018](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/CHU_KR-23_Schroeder-2018_Lernmaterial.pdf)

### Inferences
- When a figure owns an idea (e.g. a labelled pathway), the prose should *point to and interpret* the figure, not re-narrate every arrow. This matches the project's existing "don't table-ify what a figure already owns" rule.
- Summary or key-point boxes that only restate the preceding text are redundant re-reading for learners who understood it. Their value is as a retrieval or review scaffold (see the summaries section).
- Expertise reversal is directly relevant to medical students. By later years they hold biochemistry and physiology schemas, so heavy glossing or step-by-step restatement of material they already know may slow and distract them.
- Practical mitigations:
  - collapsible "refresher" callouts for prerequisite material, rather than inline re-explanation;
  - a one-line gloss rather than a paragraph;
  - letting more advanced readers skip.

### Gaps
- I found no effect-size meta-analysis of the expertise-reversal effect in the sources retrieved.
- There is no direct experimental evidence on end-of-section summary boxes as a redundancy problem.

## Advance organisers, learning objectives and pre-questions

### Takeaway
Advance organisers have small positive effects. Luiten, Ames & Ackerson (1980) is the classic meta-analysis, but I could not retrieve its effect size; earlier box-score reviews were inconclusive. Pre-questions give a moderate boost (g ≈ 0.54) to learning of the *specific prequestioned content* but essentially nothing (g ≈ 0.04) for other material in the text (St. Hilaire et al. 2024 preregistered meta-analysis). Learning objectives function similarly: they focus attention on what they name. Evidence for objectives in modern adult text studies is thin in what I retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- **Luiten, Ames & Ackerson (1980), "A meta-analysis of the effects of advance organizers on learning and retention", *American Educational Research Journal* 17(2), 211–218.** The paper exists. The search reported DOI 10.2307/1162483, a JSTOR-style DOI that I could not confirm and that may differ from the SAGE AERJ DOI, so it is omitted. Secondary sources say it countered the earlier box-score review by Barnes & Clawson (1975), which concluded that organisers "generally do not facilitate learning". — [Kabarak Journal article citing it](https://journals.kabarak.ac.ke/index.php/kjri/article/download/129/128/127). I could **not** confirm the paper's effect size. Commonly cited figures should be checked against the paper.
- An updated meta-analysis, "Thirty years after Ausubel: An updated meta-analysis of advance organizer research" (University of Illinois IDEALS), says the first 20 years (1960–1980) of advance-organiser research gave inconclusive results. — [IDEALS record (403 on fetch; summary via search)](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405). Authors and year not confirmed (likely Stone, 1983).
- **Hartley & Davies (1976), "Preinstructional strategies: The role of pretests, behavioral objectives, overviews and advance organizers", *Review of Educational Research* 46(2), 239–265.** This is the classic comparative review; its conclusions were not retrieved. — [bibliographic records](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/00346543048002251). Note: this URL is a different RER article that cites it. No DOI confirmed for Hartley & Davies.
- **St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn (2024), preregistered meta-analysis of the prequestion effect, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*.** g = 0.54 (k = 97) for prequestioned content and g = 0.04 (k = 91) for non-prequestioned content. Moderator analyses partly support an attention-based account. — [NSF PAR open-access copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812). DOI not confirmed.
- **Pan & Carpenter (2023), "Prequestioning and pretesting effects: A review of empirical research, theoretical perspectives, and implications for educational practice", *Educational Psychology Review*.** This is the main narrative review. — [Ollie Lovell summary](https://www.ollielovell.com/how-you-can-use-prequestioning-to-boost-student-learning/). DOI not confirmed. A [critical blog](https://educationrickshaw.com/why-im-skeptical-about-pre-testing/) argues that many studies lack a strong comparison condition and that opportunity cost is ignored.
- In Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), including learning objectives did not moderate the seductive-details harm, per one summary. — [QIC-WD summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Seductive%20Details%20in%20Training%20041821.pdf)

### Inferences
- Pre-questions and objectives work as **attention spotlights**: they help what they point to and do not help, or may slightly divert attention from, what they do not. Learning objectives and opening questions should therefore cover the section's core ideas, not peripheral facts.
- An advance organiser is best written as a short, higher-level conceptual frame that links new content to what students already know (Ausubel's intent), e.g. "this chapter applies the energy-balance logic from Ch. X to…". A content list is not an advance organiser. Expect at most a small benefit.
- The headings-vs-previews result (headings draw attention better) suggests an opening overview paragraph is a weaker signal than good headings. Do both, but do not rely on the preview alone.

### Gaps
- No verified effect size for Luiten et al. 1980.
- No modern meta-analysis of instructional objectives in adult text learning was found in this session.

## Worked examples and concrete examples; placement relative to principles

### Takeaway
For novices, studying worked examples beats unaided problem solving, with a medium benefit in the latest meta-analysis (Barbieri et al. 2023, mostly school mathematics). The benefit fades or reverses with expertise, which is expertise reversal again. Renkl's example-based learning theory supplies the design principles, but I did not retrieve its text. I found no strong meta-analytic evidence on whether examples should come before or after the stated principle in prose.

### Cited Findings
- **Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023), "A meta-analysis of the worked examples effect on mathematics performance", *Educational Psychology Review*.**
  - 43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes.
  - Worked examples outperformed problem-solving controls with a medium benefit, about 18 percentile points per a University of Delaware summary.
  - Correct-solution examples beat incorrect ones.
  - Adding self-explanation prompts was associated with *lower* gains in this dataset.
  - Mostly middle-school algebra and geometry.

  — [Kent State record](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance); [Learning Scientists guest post](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2024/1/25-1). DOI 10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1 per secondary source. Volume and issue are cited inconsistently. The pooled g was not confirmed.
- **Renkl (2014), "Toward an instructionally oriented theory of example-based learning", *Cognitive Science* 38(1), 1–37.** Integrates research on worked examples, observational learning and analogical reasoning into descriptive and prescriptive sub-theories: when examples are effective, phases of skill acquisition, and instructional principles. — [Scinapse record](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/1986285750). DOI not confirmed. The specific principles were not retrieved.
- Self-explanation was among the largest effects in the Mayer-corpus meta-analysis. — [Cromley & Chen 2025](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927). This conflicts in direction with Barbieri's moderator finding for math worked examples.
- Expertise reversal applies: worked examples help novices but can become redundant for experts. — [Kalyuga et al. 2003, UOW](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136)

### Inferences
- For a nutrition/medicine text, the "worked example" is a fully worked clinical or quantitative case, e.g. calculating energy requirements, interpreting a lab panel, or applying a guideline to a patient. Each step should be shown and labelled with the principle it uses. Prose on its own states a principle with no application.
- A defensible placement rule is: principle → worked example immediately after, then a faded or practice item (e.g. the existing MCQ cases). Principle and example should be adjacent (contiguity), with the example tightly tied to the objective (coherence).
- The evidence on whether examples should *precede* or *follow* the rule was not established here.

### Gaps
- No verified meta-analysis on worked examples in health-science or text-only domains. The example-before-vs-after-principle question is unresolved in the sources retrieved.
- The concreteness-fading literature was not searched.

## Summaries, key-point boxes and retrieval practice

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice (self-testing) is one of the best-supported learning techniques: g ≈ 0.50 vs restudy (Rowland 2014), g = 0.61 overall and g = 0.67 in classrooms (Adesope et al. 2017). It is rated "high utility" by Dunlosky et al. (2013). Rereading, highlighting and (student-generated) summarisation are rated "low utility". The evidence therefore favours ending sections with retrieval questions, ideally with feedback, over relying on summary boxes that are only re-read. Testing loses to restudy on an immediate test but wins on delayed tests (Roediger & Karpicke 2006).

### Cited Findings
- **Roediger & Karpicke (2006), "Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention", *Psychological Science* 17(3), 249–255.** Students read prose passages, then either took free-recall tests without feedback or restudied. On a 5-minute test, restudy won. On 2-day and 1-week tests, prior testing produced substantially greater retention. Restudying inflated students' confidence. — [WashU profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/test-enhanced-learning-taking-memory-tests-improves-long-term-ret/); [WashU news release](https://source.washu.edu/2006/03/repeated-testtaking-better-for-retention-than-repeated-studying-research-shows/). Exact percentages not retrieved. DOI not confirmed.
- **Rowland (2014), "The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: A meta-analytic review of the testing effect", *Psychological Bulletin* 140(6), 1432–1463.**
  - 61 studies, 159 effects; mean g = 0.50 vs restudy.
  - Larger effects with feedback and with retention intervals of at least one day.
  - Recall-type initial tests beat recognition, and short-answer beat multiple-choice.
  - In lab studies without corrective feedback there was no testing effect when initial retrieval success was ≤50%.
  - Applied/classroom research was excluded.

  — [Greving & Richter 2018, *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:2412](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full) (DOI confirmed: 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412); [EPFL-hosted PDF of Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf). DOI 10.1037/a0037559 per secondary source.
- **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), "Rethinking the use of tests: A meta-analysis of practice testing", *Review of Educational Research* 87(3), 659–701.**
  - 118 articles, 272 effects, ~15,472 participants (secondary figures).
  - Practice tests beat restudying and all other comparison conditions.
  - Moderated by test features, participant and study characteristics, and outcome type.
  - Classroom effect g = 0.67.
  - Multiple-choice practice produced *stronger* effects than short-answer, the reverse of Rowland. Matching practice and final test formats increased the effect.

  — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306) (DOI confirmed: 10.3102/0034654316689306); [Greving & Richter 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full). Greving & Richter give the range across the Phelps, Rowland and Adesope meta-analyses as "0.50 to 0.61". The overall g = 0.61 for Adesope et al. is inferred from that range and from memory, so confirm it in the paper.
- **Other meta-analyses of the testing effect:**
  - Bangert-Drowns et al. (1991, classroom only): d = 0.54 for testing vs no testing.
  - Schwieren et al. (2017): d = 0.56 for psychology content taught in classrooms.

  — [Greving & Richter 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full)
- **Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), "Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques: Promising directions from cognitive and educational psychology", *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58.**
  - **High utility:** practice testing and distributed practice.
  - **Low utility:** summarisation, highlighting/underlining, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, and rereading.
  - Summarisation helps only under limited conditions, e.g. students trained to summarise.

  — [APS "Which study strategies make the grade?"](https://psychologicalscience.org/?p=82014); [CiteDrive record](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/improving-students-learning-with-effective-learning-techniques). DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266 per secondary record. Detail is from press coverage rather than the monograph text.
- In the Mayer-corpus meta-analysis, the "testing effect" principle was a medium significant effect. — [Cromley & Chen 2025](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)

### Inferences
- Dunlosky's "low utility" rating for summarisation concerns **students writing summaries**, not authors providing them. I found no direct evidence on author-provided end-of-section summary boxes versus end-of-section retrieval questions. The strong evidence for retrieval versus the weak evidence for rereading implies that a summary box that is only re-read adds little.
- Converting key-point boxes into short-answer or MCQ "check yourself" prompts, with the answer or summary revealed *after* an attempt, captures the testing effect while still providing the summary as feedback. Feedback roughly doubles the effect per secondary Rowland figures (unverified).
- On question format, Rowland found short-answer beat MCQ and Adesope et al. found MCQ beat short-answer. Matching practice format to the eventual assessment format is the safer rule; mixing formats is defensible.
- The "restudy wins immediately, testing wins later" result explains why students prefer summaries and rereading, which feel more fluent. The authoring guide can say so explicitly.

### Gaps
- No experiment found that directly compares author-provided summaries with retrieval questions at the end of textbook sections.
- Exact Roediger & Karpicke percentages, Adesope's overall g, and Rowland's feedback moderator values were not confirmed in primary text.

## Spatial contiguity / split attention

### Takeaway
Placing explanatory text physically next to, or integrated into, the diagram it describes produces a medium-to-large learning gain. Schroeder & Cenkci (2018) found g = 0.63 over 58 comparisons; the earlier Ginns (2006) estimate was g = 0.72. The effect is largest for static 2D diagrams, worksheets and paper-like materials, and adult learners. It holds in anatomy/medicine (g = 0.52). This is one of the best-supported and cheapest principles to implement.

### Cited Findings
- **Schroeder & Cenkci (2018), "Spatial contiguity and spatial split-attention effects in multimedia learning environments: A meta-analysis", *Educational Psychology Review* 30(3), 679–701.**
  - 58 comparisons, N = 2,426, studies 1989–2017. Integrated vs separated text and picture: g = 0.63.
  - By medium: worksheets g = 0.80; computer-based g = 0.50.
  - By diagram: 2D diagrams g = 0.71; static g = 0.68 vs interactive g = 0.24.
  - By content overlap: text and picture complementary without duplication g = 0.74.
  - By outcome: transfer g = 0.65; retention g = 0.39.
  - By age: adult education g = 0.72 (k = 35).
  - By subject: anatomy/medicine g = 0.52 (k = 8).
  - Learning was measured at the longest available delay.

  — [TUM Clearing House review (German PDF, read in full)](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/CHU_KR-23_Schroeder-2018_Lernmaterial.pdf); bibliographic details from [Altmetric](https://springeropen.altmetric.com/details/34917578) and [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_attention_effect). DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9 per secondary source.
- **Ginns (2006), "Integrating information: A meta-analysis of the spatial contiguity and temporal contiguity effects", *Learning and Instruction*.** g = 0.72 over 37 effects, as reported in the TUM review above. Full bibliographic details and DOI not confirmed in this session.
- Schroeder & Cenkci's 2020 follow-up in *Journal of Educational Psychology* asks whether cognitive-load measures explain the principle. — [Ovid record](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jedup/pdf/10.1037/edu0000372~do-measures-of-cognitive-load-explain-the-spatial). DOI 10.1037/edu0000372 per record. Findings not retrieved.
- "Large, consistent effects were found for text + diagrams" across factual, inferential and transfer outcomes in the Mayer corpus. — [Cromley & Chen 2025](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927)

### Inferences
- For an online Quarto book:
  - place each figure immediately beside or after the paragraph that interprets it;
  - put labels *on* the diagram rather than in a separate key where possible;
  - write captions that explain rather than merely title;
  - avoid "see Figure 3 two sections back".
- On narrow mobile screens, side-by-side layout collapses to stacking, so keep the figure and its explanatory paragraph adjacent in source order.
- The effect is strongest when text and picture *complement* each other. This reinforces the redundancy rule: prose should interpret, not duplicate, the figure.

### Gaps
- Ginns 2006 volume, issue and DOI not confirmed. No evidence retrieved on hover-tooltip or pop-up integration in online text.

## Pre-training principle (teaching key terms first; glossing)

### Takeaway
Learning the names and characteristics of key components *before* a complex causal explanation improves transfer. Mayer's tallies give median d of about 0.75–0.92 across 5–16 tests, but these are small lab experiments on mechanical and scientific systems, mostly by Mayer's group. Pre-training is categorised as a way to manage *essential* (intrinsic) load. It supports glossing or introducing terms before the mechanism paragraph rather than mid-explanation.

### Cited Findings
- **Mayer, Mathias & Wetzell (2002), "Fostering understanding of multimedia messages through pre-training: Evidence for a two-stage theory of mental model construction", *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied* 8, 147–154.** Learners who first learned the names and behaviour of the system's components (e.g. car brakes, tyre pumps) before a narrated animation of how the system works did better on transfer. The proposed mechanism is two-stage mental-model building: component models first, then the causal model. — [Cambridge "Pretraining principle" chapter listing](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/multimedia-learning/pretraining-principle/01791D57F5D4164251269E6DF56A8BF1). DOI not confirmed.
- **Mayer's own tallies:**
  - 2005 Handbook: 7/7 tests, median d = 0.92.
  - *Multimedia Learning* (2009): 5/5 tests, median d = 0.85.
  - 2014 Handbook: 13/16 tests, median d = 0.75.
  - A 12-principles review lists pre-training at d = 0.46.

  — [Cambridge Handbook chapter "Principles for managing essential processing… segmenting, pretraining, and modality"](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/4110A2275F6DCD02DAB1F8B37BA7E5CE); figures via search summaries of these chapters and the [synapsesocial abstract](https://www.synapsesocial.com/es/papers/69dbbbdacebd566818835a69).
- Pre-training interacts with prior knowledge. It is meant for learners who lack the component knowledge, consistent with expertise reversal. — [Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136) (theoretical inference; no direct pre-training × expertise meta-analysis found)

### Inferences
- For the book, introduce and briefly define the key players before the paragraph that runs the mechanism. For example: name and describe insulin, GLUT4 and hepatic glucose output in a short lead-in or a compact term list, then explain the causal chain. Avoid defining terms inside the causal sentence, which splits attention between learning a term and following the chain.
- This supports the existing "one-line just-in-time gloss" house rule, with a refinement: glosses should come *just before* the explanation that depends on them, not inside it.
- A glossary page (the project has an untracked `glossary.qmd`) is a reasonable pre-training resource. Inline links are better than end-of-book lookups, given spatial contiguity.
- For students who already know the terms, pre-training is redundant. Collapsible definitions avoid penalising them.

### Gaps
- Pre-training evidence is narrow: few labs, short mechanical or scientific lessons, mostly narrated animation rather than text.
- No independent meta-analysis of pre-training was found. The d values are Mayer's medians, not meta-analytic weighted means.
- No evidence was found specifically on glossaries or inline definitions in textbooks.
