# Medical-education evidence on designing readable, learnable written/online instructional material for medical students

Verification note: every PMID and DOI below was confirmed this session by pulling the PubMed record (NCBI E-utilities efetch, 2026-10-21) and checking title, authors, journal, year and pages against it. Links go to the PubMed record. Abstract-level detail only. Full texts of Mayer 2010 and the Wiley-hosted papers were paywalled (HTTP 403), so the claims about their contents come from the abstracts.

## 1. Cognitive load theory (CLT) applied to medical education: design principles for written material

### Takeaway
The core health-professions CLT sources (van Merriënboer & Sweller 2010; AMEE Guide 86; Leppink & van den Heuvel 2015) agree on three moves. (1) Cut extraneous load: integrate split sources, remove redundancy, use worked examples. (2) Manage intrinsic load: order material simple-to-complex and pre-teach the elements. (3) Fade support as expertise grows (the expertise-reversal effect), because what helps novices can hinder advanced learners. Leppink warns explicitly that "lower load is always better" is a misconception.

### Cited Findings
- van Merriënboer & Sweller (2010), "Cognitive load theory in health professional education: design principles and strategies", *Med Educ* 44(1):85-93, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03498.x, PMID 20078759. This is a narrative/theoretical review. Its framing: working memory is limited and long-term memory holds schemas. Learning means building and automating schemas. — [PubMed 20078759](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20078759/)
  - It sets out **15 design guidelines**. The ones that reduce extraneous load are goal-free tasks, worked examples, completion tasks, integrating different sources of information (the split-attention principle), using multiple modalities, and reducing redundancy. — [PubMed 20078759](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20078759/)
  - Intrinsic load is managed by **simple-to-complex ordering** and by moving from low- to high-fidelity environments. Germane load is optimised by variability over tasks, contextual interference and **evoking self-explanation**. — [PubMed 20078759](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20078759/)
  - The **expertise reversal effect**: "well-designed instruction for novice learners is different from instruction for more experienced learners." — [PubMed 20078759](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20078759/)
- Young, van Merriënboer, Durning & ten Cate (2014), "Cognitive Load Theory: implications for medical education: AMEE Guide No. 86", *Med Teach* 36(5):371-84, DOI 10.3109/0142159X.2014.889290, PMID 24593808. — [PubMed 24593808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24593808/)
  - Working memory is a "bottleneck". When total load exceeds its capacity, performance and learning are impaired. Recommended techniques: decrease extraneous load (e.g. worked examples), "titrate intrinsic load to the developmental stage of the learner (e.g. simplify task without decontextualizing)", and direct spare capacity to germane cognitive strategies. — [PubMed 24593808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24593808/)
  - Medical content has high "element interactivity": many knowledge sets must be integrated at once, so it can exceed novice working-memory capacity. The guide explicitly covers **self-directed learning** settings as well as classroom and workplace. — [PubMed 24593808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24593808/)
  - Expertise reversal: techniques that help early learners "may not help and may even interfere with learning among more advanced learners." — [PubMed 24593808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24593808/)
- Leppink & van den Heuvel (2015), "The evolution of cognitive load theory and its application to medical education", *Perspect Med Educ* 4(3):119-27, DOI 10.1007/s40037-015-0192-x, PMID 26016429 (open access, PMC4456454). — [PubMed 26016429](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26016429/)
  - It warns against "misconceptions such as lower cognitive load always being beneficial to learning". — [PubMed 26016429](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26016429/)
  - Its holistic model has three dimensions: **task fidelity** (literature is the lowest fidelity, then simulated patients, then real patients); **task complexity** (the number of information elements); and **instructional support** (worked examples are the most support, then completion tasks, then autonomous performance). The prescription: start with high support on low-fidelity, low-complexity tasks and fade the support, then repeat at higher complexity, then at higher fidelity. — [PubMed 26016429](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26016429/)
- Leppink & Duvivier (2016), "Twelve tips for medical curriculum design from a cognitive load theory perspective", *Med Teach* 38(7):669-74, DOI 10.3109/0142159X.2015.1132829, PMID 26806279. It gives four tips each for fidelity, complexity and support, built on the same high-to-low support trajectory. — [PubMed 26806279](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26806279/)
- Grunwald & Corsbie-Massay (2006), "Guidelines for cognitively efficient multimedia learning tools: educational strategies, cognitive load, and interface design", *Acad Med* 81(3):213-23, PMID 16501261. A literature review (PubMed search, 2004). It found that "techniques common to existing MMLTs often neglect to account for theories of cognitive load and may be detrimental to the learning process." — [PubMed 16501261](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16501261/)

### Inferences
- A textbook "literature" chapter is the lowest-fidelity rung in Leppink's model. That is the right place for high instructional support: worked examples, explicit signposting, and element-by-element build-up before integration.
- "Dense, hard to read" in CLT terms most likely means high element interactivity is presented all at once, plus extraneous load from split sources and redundancy (e.g. a figure and a paragraph that each say half of the story). The remedies are to pre-teach components (glosses, pre-training), sequence from simple to complex, and integrate text with figures, rather than simply cutting content.
- Expertise reversal argues for layered design: a scaffolded core path plus optional expanders or detail for stronger students, rather than one register for everyone.

### Gaps
- None of these sources tests prose *readability* metrics (sentence length, jargon density) with medical students. They are mainly theoretical or derived from non-medical lab studies. Leppink and AMEE 86 are narrative reviews, not systematic reviews.
- I could not access the full text of van Merriënboer & Sweller 2010 to list all 15 guidelines by name. Only the categories in the abstract are given above.

## 2. Multimedia learning principles applied in medical education

### Takeaway
Mayer's principles do transfer to medical students. Two quasi-experimental cohort studies at Northwestern found that redesigning a bullet-point lecture to these principles produced large gains, including at 4 weeks (d ≈ 0.8-1.2). The evidence base in medical education is small, single-site and not randomised.

### Cited Findings
- Mayer (2010), "Applying the science of learning to medical education", *Med Educ* 44(6):543-9, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2010.03624.x, PMID 20604850. The cognitive theory of multimedia learning rests on three ideas: separate channels for words and pictures, limited working-memory capacity, and meaningful learning that requires appropriate processing. — [PubMed 20604850](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20604850/)
  - Three instructional goals: **reduce extraneous processing**, **manage essential processing**, **foster generative processing**. The paper presents "nine evidence-based principles" to achieve these. — [PubMed 20604850](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20604850/)
- Issa, Schuller, Santacaterina, Shapiro, Wang, Mayer & DaRosa (2011), "Applying multimedia design principles enhances learning in medical education", *Med Educ* 45(8):818-26, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2011.03988.x, PMID 21752078. — [PubMed 21752078](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21752078/)
  - Design: pre-test/post-test control-group design, non-randomised (allocation by clerkship quarter). Year 3 surgery clerkship students, **n=91 modified-design vs n=39 traditional**. Same lecture on shock; the slides were redesigned to Mayer's principles. — [PubMed 21752078](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21752078/)
  - Results: significantly greater improvement in retention (F=10.2, p=0.0016) and total score (F=7.13, p=0.0081). The authors concluded the principles are "easy to implement" but that the effects on transfer and long-term learning were unproven at that point. — [PubMed 21752078](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21752078/)
- Issa, Mayer, Schuller, Wang, Shapiro & DaRosa (2013), "Teaching for understanding in medical classrooms using multimedia design principles", *Med Educ* 47(4):388-96, DOI 10.1111/medu.12127, PMID 23488758. — [PubMed 23488758](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23488758/)
  - Cohorts of **37 (traditional bullet-point PowerPoint) vs 43 (redesigned)** Year 3 students; identical content. — [PubMed 23488758](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23488758/)
  - Results for the redesigned slides: delayed **transfer** d=0.83 at 1 week and d=1.17 at 4 weeks; delayed **retention** d=0.83 at 1 week and d=0.79 at 4 weeks; immediate retention d=1.49 and immediate transfer d=0.76. — [PubMed 23488758](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23488758/)
- Ruiz, Cook & Levinson (2009), "Computer animations in medical education: a critical literature review", *Med Educ* 43(9):838-46, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03429.x, PMID 19709008. Few comparative medical-education studies exist and the evidence is inconclusive. Non-medical research comparing animations with static images is conflicting. Features that appear to help: **learner control of pace**, interactivity, and **segmenting** into small chunks. Benefits vary with prior knowledge and spatial ability. — [PubMed 19709008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19709008/)

### Inferences
- The Issa studies tested slides, not textbook prose. The comparison condition, though (bullet-point text-heavy slides), is close to the "dense" problem. The redesign principles that generalise to online text are coherence (cut the extraneous), signalling (cue the structure and key ideas), integrating words with relevant graphics, segmenting and pre-training.
- Animations and interactive figures are not automatically better than static figures. If used, give learners control of pace and segment them.

### Gaps
- **Unverified:** I could not open the full text of Mayer 2010 (Wiley returned 403), so I cannot list its nine principles verbatim. In Mayer's wider work, the extraneous-processing principles are typically coherence, signalling, redundancy, spatial contiguity and temporal contiguity; the essential-processing ones are segmenting, pre-training and modality; the generative ones are multimedia and personalisation. Whether the 2010 paper's nine are exactly this set (minus one) is not confirmed, so the report writer should not attribute a specific list to Mayer 2010.
- I found no medical-education RCT that applies multimedia principles to *textbook or online-chapter prose* specifically. Both Issa studies are single-institution, non-randomised cohort comparisons with modest samples.

## 3. How medical students actually read and study

### Takeaway
Students lean heavily on instructor notes, question banks and third-party condensed resources. Recommended textbook chapters are accessed far less than lecture notes. Higher-stakes exam performance is associated with practice questions and spaced flashcards, not with textbook reading. Two exceptions: one Australian survey found textbooks still among the most-used resources for *new* material, and language barriers deter some students from textbooks. Taken together, a textbook competes with condensed, question-driven resources and has to earn its reading time.

### Cited Findings
- Judd & Elliott (2017), "Selection and use of online learning resources by first-year medical students: cross-sectional study", *JMIR Med Educ* 3(2):e17, DOI 10.2196/mededu.7382, PMID 28970187 (Melbourne; case-based curriculum). Data came from a survey (118 complete responses, 36.2% response rate) plus learning-platform logs for an entire semester (2012 cohort, n=337). — [PubMed 28970187](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28970187/)
  - About 70,000 resource accesses, "overwhelmingly" lecture notes, each accessed **167 times** on average. Recommended journal articles averaged **49** and online textbook chapters **31**. A quarter of students accessed very few resources through the platform. — [PubMed 28970187](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28970187/)
  - Google and Wikipedia were the most-used non-university sites. Students chose resources on **peers'** recommendation more than staff's. — [PubMed 28970187](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28970187/)
- Wynter, Burgess, Kalman, Heron & Bleasel (2019), "Medical students: what educational resources are they using?", *BMC Med Educ* 19:36, DOI 10.1186/s12909-019-1462-9, PMID 30683084. Two Australian universities; 350/1083 responded (32.3%). — [PubMed 30683084](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30683084/)
  - "Making written notes and reading textbooks were the most frequently utilized resources for learning new material". Question banks were the most-used resource for revision. 92% used online teaching videos and 90.6% used question banks. The authors flag that question banks may be poorly aligned with the curriculum. — [PubMed 30683084](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30683084/)
  - This partly contradicts the Judd & Elliott log data: self-report puts textbooks high, while platform logs show textbook chapters accessed rarely. — [Wynter 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30683084/); [Judd & Elliott 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28970187/)
- Burk-Rafel, Santen & Purkiss (2017), "Study behaviors and USMLE Step 1 performance: implications of a student self-directed parallel curriculum", *Acad Med* 92(11S):S67-S74, DOI 10.1097/ACM.0000000000001916, PMID 29065026. University of Michigan; 274/332 participated (82.5%). — [PubMed 29065026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29065026/)
  - During the dedicated study period students averaged 11.0 h/day over 35.3 days. They read an exam-specific **700-page review book** on average 2.1 times and completed on average **3,597 practice MCQs**. More review-book use and more practice questions were associated with higher Step 1 scores after adjustment (adjusted R² 0.56). The authors describe a self-directed "parallel curriculum" built from third-party resources. — [PubMed 29065026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29065026/)
- Deng, Gluckstein & Larsen (2015), "Student-directed retrieval practice is a predictor of medical licensing examination performance", *Perspect Med Educ* 4(6):308-13, DOI 10.1007/s40037-015-0220-x, PMID 26498443. n=72, one institution. — [PubMed 26498443](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26498443/)
  - All students used practice MCQs (mean 3,870); 31% used Anki. MCQs completed and unique Anki cards seen independently predicted Step 1 score. Each extra 445 questions or 1,700 Anki cards was associated with roughly one extra Step 1 point. Pre-made commercial flashcards (Firecracker) did not predict the score. — [PubMed 26498443](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26498443/)
- Jameel, Gazzaz, Baig et al. (2019), "Medical students' preferences towards learning resources and their study habits at King Abdulaziz University, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia", *BMC Res Notes* 12:30, DOI 10.1186/s13104-019-4052-3, PMID 30654842. 347/500 students. **One-third** said they do not readily understand textbooks because of limited English, and 19.3% showed a lack of interest in textbooks. Male students mostly preferred instructor handouts. — [PubMed 30654842](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30654842/)

### Inferences
- Students treat the instructor-curated core (lecture notes or handouts) and question banks as the "real" curriculum. An online textbook that wants to be read needs to look like the core: concise, aligned to assessment, scannable, and with built-in questions.
- Burk-Rafel shows students *will* read long condensed texts repeatedly (a 700-page review book about twice) when the text is perceived as high-yield and aligned with the exam. So length is not the barrier in itself; perceived yield and efficiency matter.
- For a Singapore audience studying in English, the Saudi finding is a reminder that linguistic density (long sentences, rare words) is a barrier in its own right for non-native or bilingual readers. This is an inference; that study is not Singapore-specific.

### Gaps
- I found no large, multi-school, longitudinal study documenting a *decline* in textbook reading by medical students over time. The evidence is cross-sectional and single- or dual-institution, with low response rates (32-36%) in two of the surveys.
- I found no eye-tracking or reading-behaviour study (scanning vs reading, dwell time) of medical students on online textbook pages.

## 4. Test-enhanced learning and retrieval practice: implications for in-chapter quizzes

### Takeaway
This is the strongest evidence base in this set of notes. Several RCTs in medical trainees and a BEME systematic review show that repeated retrieval with feedback beats re-studying for long-term retention, at 6 months and longer. Effortful recall (short answer) beats recognition, spacing helps, and feedback is essential. Embedded self-assessment questions in web modules improve scores and are preferred by learners.

### Cited Findings
- Larsen, Butler & Roediger (2008), "Test-enhanced learning in medical education", *Med Educ* 42(10):959-66, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2008.03124.x, PMID 18823514. A review. Its conclusions: test often and spaced over time; questions requiring **effortful recall** (e.g. short answer) produce bigger gains than recognition (MCQ); "feedback is crucial". — [PubMed 18823514](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18823514/)
- Larsen, Butler & Roediger (2009), "Repeated testing improves long-term retention relative to repeated study: a randomised controlled trial", *Med Educ* 43(12):1174-81, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03518.x, PMID 19930508. Paediatric and EM residents; crossover by topic (19 + 21 completers). Short-answer tests with feedback, given 3 times about 2 weeks apart, compared with studying an *identical* review sheet. At **6 months or more**: 39% vs 26% (d=0.91). — [PubMed 19930508](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19930508/)
- Larsen, Butler & Roediger (2013), "Comparative effects of test-enhanced learning and self-explanation on long-term retention", *Med Educ* 47(7):674-82, DOI 10.1111/medu.12141, PMID 23746156. 47 Year 1 medical students, four conditions within subjects, final test at 6 months. — [PubMed 23746156](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23746156/)
  - Testing beat studying (η²=0.33). Self-explanation also helped but less (η²=0.08). Scores: testing + explanation 40%, testing 36%, study + self-explanation 29%, study alone 20%. — [PubMed 23746156](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23746156/)
- Green, Moeller & Spak (2018), "Test-enhanced learning in health professions education: a systematic review: BEME Guide No. 48", *Med Teach* 40(4):337-50, DOI 10.1080/0142159X.2018.1430354, PMID 29390949. 19 controlled studies, 41 outcomes. — [PubMed 29390949](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29390949/)
  - Five of six immediate-learning outcomes (SMD 0.09-0.44), **21 of 23 retention outcomes (SMD 0.12-2.5)** and **all seven transfer outcomes (SMD 0.33-1.1)** favoured test-enhanced learning over studying. Formats included short-answer questions, MCQs, simulation and standardised patients. "Robust effects across health professions, learners, TEL formats, and learning outcomes." — [PubMed 29390949](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29390949/)
- Kerfoot, DeWolf, Masser, Church & Federman (2007), "Spaced education improves the retention of clinical knowledge by medical students: a randomised controlled trial", *Med Educ* 41(1):23-31, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2929.2006.02644.x, PMID 17209889. Weekly emailed MCQ case scenarios, each followed by the answer, a teaching-point summary and explanations. 95/133 students completed the end-of-year test. Composite scores improved (p<0.001), with the largest effect for students 6-8 months out from the rotation (d=1.01). — [PubMed 17209889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17209889/)
- Cook, Thompson, Thomas, Thomas & Pankratz (2006), "Impact of self-assessment questions and learning styles in Web-based learning: a randomized, controlled, crossover trial", *Acad Med* 81(3):231-8, PMID 16501263. 121 IM residents. Web modules **with embedded case-based self-assessment questions** scored 78.9% vs 76.2% without them (p=0.006). 83% preferred the question format; 71% rated it more efficient even though it took longer (60 vs 44 min). Learning styles did not predict preference. — [PubMed 16501263](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16501263/)
- Cook, Levinson, Garside, Dupras, Erwin & Montori (2010), "Instructional design variations in internet-based learning for health professions education: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Acad Med* 85(5):909-22, DOI 10.1097/ACM.0b013e3181d6c319, PMID 20520049. 51 studies (30 RCTs). — [PubMed 20520049](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20520049/)
  - Pooled effect sizes associated with better learning: high vs low **interactivity** 0.27 (15 studies); **practice exercises** 0.40 (10 studies); **feedback** 0.68 (only 2 studies, CI 0.01-1.35); **repetition** 0.19 (2 studies). Online discussion was not significant. Inconsistency was large (I² ≥ 89%). — [PubMed 20520049](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20520049/)
- Cook et al. (2008), "Internet-based learning in the health professions: a meta-analysis", *JAMA* 300(10):1181-96, DOI 10.1001/jama.300.10.1181, PMID 18780847. 201 studies. Compared with no intervention, internet-based learning had a large effect on knowledge (ES 1.00). Compared with non-internet formats the effect was small (knowledge ES 0.12). In other words, the medium itself matters little; the design does. — [PubMed 18780847](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18780847/)

### Inferences
- In-chapter quizzes are the best-supported feature an online textbook can add. To maximise benefit: put questions inside or right after each section rather than only at the end; give elaborated feedback (why each option is right or wrong); include some effortful-recall or short-answer prompts as well as MCQs; and resurface items later (spacing), for example in end-of-part reviews.
- Kerfoot's format (a short case, an MCQ, then the answer, a teaching-point summary and an explanation) is a validated template for case-framed in-chapter questions.
- Larsen 2013 suggests that prompting self-explanation ("explain why…") adds value on top of testing.

### Gaps
- Most of these RCTs are small (n≈40-120), from a few US institutions, and several involve residents rather than Year 1-2 students. The BEME review pools only 19 studies.
- I found no study that tests the optimal *density or placement* of quiz questions within a written chapter in a medical context. The Cook 2010 feedback estimate rests on only 2 studies.

## 5. Formats: summaries, tables, algorithms or flowcharts, concept maps, and causal/basic-science explanation

### Takeaway
Direct medical-education evidence comparing text formats (bullets vs prose vs tables vs flowcharts) for learning is thin. The concept-map literature is mostly descriptive. One consistent strand of experimental evidence (Woods and colleagues) shows that teaching the **causal mechanism**, rather than feature lists or structured algorithms alone, produces more coherent, durable diagnostic knowledge. That is directly relevant to keeping biochemistry and physiology explanation in a nutrition textbook while making it readable.

### Cited Findings
- Daley & Torre (2010), "Concept maps in medical education: an analytical literature review", *Med Educ* 44(5):440-8, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2010.03628.x, PMID 20374475. 35 studies (conceptual and empirical). Concept maps functioned to (i) promote meaningful learning, (ii) provide an additional learning resource, (iii) enable instructor feedback, and (iv) support assessment. Newer uses include serial concept maps and maps to help "learners with lower cognitive competence". — [PubMed 20374475](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20374475/)
- Torre, Durning & Daley (2013), "Twelve tips for teaching with concept maps in medical education", *Med Teach* 35(3):201-8, DOI 10.3109/0142159X.2013.759644, PMID 23464896. Experience-based tips (low evidence level). Maps can reveal misconceptions and knowledge gaps. — [PubMed 23464896](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23464896/)
- Woods, Howey, Brooks & Norman (2006), "Speed kills? Speed, accuracy, encapsulations and causal understanding", *Med Educ* 40(10):973-9, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2929.2006.02556.x, PMID 16987187. Undergraduate psychology students learning four pseudo-endocrine diseases. Learning causal explanations as well as features gave better recognition of encapsulated concepts (50% vs 41%) and better diagnosis under time pressure (71% vs 66%), but slightly worse under "thoroughness" instructions (67% vs 73%). — [PubMed 16987187](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16987187/)
- Woods (2007), "Science is fundamental: the role of biomedical knowledge in clinical reasoning", *Med Educ* 41(12):1173-7, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2007.02911.x, PMID 18045369. A review. Biomedical knowledge helps novices build "a coherent and stable mental representation of disease categories", supporting retention over time. Teachers "should attempt to make explicit connections between biomedical knowledge and clinical facts". — [PubMed 18045369](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18045369/)
- Baghdady, Pharoah, Regehr, Lam & Woods (2009), "The role of basic sciences in diagnostic oral radiology", *J Dent Educ* 73(10):1187-93, PMID 19805783. A randomised comparison in dental and dental-hygiene students (n=96). **Basic-science (pathophysiology) explanation outperformed both a structured algorithm and an unstructured feature list** on diagnostic tests, immediately and at 1 week. Memory tests did not differ. The authors interpret this as supporting "conceptual coherence" rather than mere organisation. — [PubMed 19805783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19805783/)

### Inferences
- Flowcharts and algorithms or tables are useful *organisers*, but on this evidence they do not replace causal explanation. The best format is probably a mechanism narrative plus a summarising table or figure, not one in place of the other.
- Concept maps have stronger support as *learner-generated* activities and feedback tools than as static author-supplied figures. A pre-drawn map in a chapter is closer to a diagram, where the multimedia principles (signalling, coherence, contiguity) apply.

### Gaps
- I found no medical-education RCT that directly compares bulleted summaries vs continuous prose vs tables in a textbook or module for learning outcomes. This is a real gap in the medical-education literature, and general educational-psychology evidence would have to stand in.
- The Woods studies use artificial diseases and often non-medical participants (psychology undergraduates) or dental students. Their generalisability to nutrition content is plausible but untested.
- A 2026 nursing meta-analysis on concept mapping and critical thinking turned up in search (*Int J Nurs Educ Scholarsh*), but I did not read it and it is not medical-student-specific.

## 6. Worked examples, illness scripts and case-based framing for novices

### Takeaway
Two German RCTs show that case-based worked examples help medical students acquire diagnostic knowledge, especially worked examples that include errors and come with *elaborated* feedback. Bare correct/incorrect feedback on erroneous examples harms learning. Illness-script theory justifies structuring disease content as enabling conditions → pathophysiological fault → consequences. The BEME review of case-based learning finds that students love it but the outcome evidence is inconclusive.

### Cited Findings
- Kopp, Stark & Fischer (2008), "Fostering diagnostic knowledge through computer-supported, case-based worked examples: effects of erroneous examples and feedback", *Med Educ* 42(8):823-9, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2008.03122.x, PMID 18564096. RCT, n=153, 2×2 design (errors vs none; elaborated vs knowledge-of-correct-result feedback). Diagnostic knowledge was supported mainly by erroneous examples combined with elaborated feedback. The effects were sustained, as shown on a regular MCQ exam (subgroup n=52 vs 145 controls). — [PubMed 18564096](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18564096/)
- Kopp, Stark, Kühne-Eversmann & Fischer (2009), "Do worked examples foster medical students' diagnostic knowledge of hyperthyroidism?", *Med Educ* 43(12):1210-7, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2009.03531.x, PMID 19930513. Replication RCT, n=124, same 2×2 design. Erroneous examples plus elaborated feedback was best. **Erroneous examples with knowledge-of-correct-result feedback impaired learning.** The effects were independent of prior knowledge and time on task. — [PubMed 19930513](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19930513/)
- Chamberland, St-Onge, Setrakian et al. (2011), "The influence of medical students' self-explanations on diagnostic performance", *Med Educ* 45(7):688-95, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2011.03933.x, PMID 21649701. 36 Year 3 students, randomised. Self-explanation while solving cases improved diagnostic performance one week later, but **only for the less familiar topic**. — [PubMed 21649701](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21649701/)
- Charlin, Boshuizen, Custers & Feltovich (2007), "Scripts and clinical reasoning", *Med Educ* 41(12):1178-84, DOI 10.1111/j.1365-2923.2007.02924.x, PMID 18045370. An essay on illness scripts as goal-directed knowledge structures that generate expectations about features and guide classification and action. — [PubMed 18045370](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18045370/)
- Custers (2015), "Thirty years of illness scripts: theoretical origins and practical applications", *Med Teach* 37(5):457-62, DOI 10.3109/0142159X.2014.956052, PMID 25180878. The concept has "solid underpinnings" and can inform clinical-teaching recommendations. — [PubMed 25180878](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25180878/)
- Thistlethwaite, Davies, Ekeocha et al. (2012), "The effectiveness of case-based learning in health professional education. A BEME systematic review: BEME Guide No. 23", *Med Teach* 34(6):e421-44, DOI 10.3109/0142159X.2012.680939, PMID 22578051. 104 papers, 23 of higher quality, synthesised narratively. Students and teachers enjoy case-based learning, but "the empirical data taken as a whole are inconclusive as to the effects on learning compared with other types of activity." — [PubMed 22578051](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22578051/)

### Inferences
- For a textbook, cases work best as worked examples with reasoning made explicit ("here is how a clinician thinks through this"), followed by practice. Common-error examples ("a student might conclude X; here is why that's wrong") help, provided the explanation is elaborated rather than just the right answer.
- Case vignettes boost engagement (BEME 23), but engagement alone is not evidence of learning. Pair cases with retrieval questions and explicit mechanism.
- Self-explanation prompts pay off most on unfamiliar material, which describes most of a nutrition or biochemistry course for first- and second-year students.

### Gaps
- Worked-example RCTs in medicine are few and come from one German research group, using computer-based diagnostic cases rather than prose textbooks.

## 7. Chunked and microlearning formats: evidence and limits

### Takeaway
Microlearning (units under 15 minutes) is popular and well liked, but the health-professions evidence is mostly reactions and short-term knowledge. A 2019 scoping review found 17 studies, with none reaching patient or practice outcomes, and it noted downsides. Segmenting within a coherent narrative is better supported, through the multimedia and animation literature, than atomising content into disconnected fragments.

### Cited Findings
- De Gagne, Park, Hall, Woodward, Yamane & Kim (2019), "Microlearning in health professions education: scoping review", *JMIR Med Educ* 5(2):e13997, DOI 10.2196/13997, PMID 31339105. 3,096 records screened, 17 included (2011-2018). All featured units under 15 minutes and content aggregation. 94% assessed reactions (Kirkpatrick level 1), 82% knowledge or skill (level 2), 29% behaviour (level 3), and none level 4. Reported positives: knowledge, confidence and retention. Downsides: "pedagogical discomfort, technology inequalities, and privacy concerns." — [PubMed 31339105](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31339105/)
- Ruiz, Cook & Levinson (2009): **segmenting** and learner control of pace appear to aid learning from animations. — [PubMed 19709008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19709008/)
- Woods (2007) and Baghdady et al. (2009): coherence, meaning causal connection between elements, drives durable diagnostic knowledge. Isolated feature lists underperform. — [PubMed 18045369](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18045369/); [PubMed 19805783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19805783/)
- Spaced, short, case-plus-question units (Kerfoot 2007) improved retention in an RCT. These were reinforcement units after initial teaching, not the primary instruction. — [PubMed 17209889](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17209889/)

### Inferences
- "Chunk" a chapter into short, headed segments, each with a single job and a retrieval check, while keeping a visible connecting narrative so the causal chain survives. Microlearning is best supported as *spaced reinforcement* of already-taught content, not as a replacement for integrated explanation.
- Over-fragmenting risks exactly what the Woods coherence studies warn against: memorised feature lists without the mechanism that makes them stick.

### Gaps
- I found no medical-education RCT that compares a microlearning version of primary textbook content with an integrated chapter on delayed retention or transfer.
- The scoping review is dominated by nursing, pharmacy and other disciplines, and by low Kirkpatrick levels. Its quality appraisal is limited by design (it is a scoping review).
