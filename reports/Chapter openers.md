# Chapter openers: proposed rewrites (2026-10-22)

Rule: two plain sentences under the chapter title. The first says what the topic is and why it matters to a doctor; the second says what the chapter covers, in the order of its sections. No anecdote, staged patient question, aphorism or "The clinician's task is…" formula; "we" is fine, "This chapter…" is not. Facts kept from the current opener wherever they were already there; nothing new is claimed.

## Why nutrition matters

**Now:** Most deaths worldwide now come from slow, chronic diseases shaped by daily habits, and diet is among the largest of the risks a patient can change.

**Proposed:** Most deaths worldwide now come from chronic diseases shaped by daily habits, and diet is among the largest of the risks a patient can change. We look at the burden of these diseases, how nutrition connects with the other pillars of health, the evidence for whole dietary patterns, and why counselling about food is a skill a doctor can learn.

## Energy balance

**Now:** Body weight follows the gap between the energy we take in and the energy we spend. That gap is not free to move: when intake falls for long enough, energy expenditure falls too, narrowing it.

**Proposed:** Body weight follows the balance between the energy taken in and the energy spent. We cover the parts of energy expenditure and how to estimate them, how body composition and expenditure are measured, and why expenditure falls during prolonged weight loss, which makes the loss harder to sustain.

## Macronutrients: carbohydrate, fat, protein and fibre

**Now:** Carbohydrate, fat and protein are the nutrients that supply energy. Yet two plates with the same energy content can mean very different physiology, because quality drives health as much as quantity does.

**Proposed:** Carbohydrate, fat and protein supply the body's energy, and two plates with the same energy content can have very different effects on health. We take each nutrient in turn, including the glycaemic index, fibre and the shape of fatty acids, and then show how the nutrients combine into dietary patterns.

## Micronutrients, hydration and electrolytes

**Now:** Vitamins and minerals are needed only in milligram or microgram amounts and supply almost no energy, yet a shortfall can blind a child, soften bone or leave permanent nerve damage, and an excess of some is toxic. The body also holds water and electrolytes in tight balance, and in Singapore the main problem with them is an excess: too much sodium.

**Proposed:** Vitamins and minerals are needed only in milligram or microgram amounts, yet a shortfall can blind a child, soften bone or leave permanent nerve damage, and an excess of some is toxic. We cover the vitamins, the minerals and trace elements, and water and electrolytes, where Singapore's main problem is too much sodium, and close with when a patient actually needs a supplement.

## Digestion and absorption

**Now:** A sachet of glucose and salt can rehydrate a child with severe diarrhoea, because one transporter in the gut wall keeps working while the gut pours out fluid. The nutrients in a meal enter the body by crossing that same wall, most of them only after digestion has broken them into pieces small enough to cross.

**Proposed:** Before most nutrients can enter the body, digestion has to break food into molecules small enough to cross the gut wall. We follow a meal through the gut: how it is moved, digested and timed by gut hormones, how each nutrient crosses the wall, and how this applies in practice to food form, meal order and oral rehydration.

## Integrative metabolism: the fed and fasted body

**Now:** After a meal the body must store a flood of nutrients, and between meals it must live on those stores while keeping the brain supplied. One connected network of pathways does both, thrown between storing and mobilising by hormones.

**Proposed:** After a meal the body has to store incoming nutrients, and between meals it has to live on those stores while keeping the brain supplied. We read the metabolic map, follow how insulin and glucagon switch the body between the fed, fasted and starved states, apply this to exercise, the respiratory quotient and lipoproteins, and finish with what goes wrong in diabetes, alcohol excess and hypoglycaemia.

## The gut microbiome

**Now:** The colon holds a dense community of microbes that ferments what human enzymes cannot digest, and its products fuel the colonic lining and signal to the rest of the body. Few topics in consumer nutrition carry more marketing claims, so the mechanism is the test of each one.

**Proposed:** The colon holds a dense community of microbes that ferments what human enzymes cannot digest, and its products fuel the colonic lining and signal to the rest of the body. We cover how the microbiome is composed and develops, what it does for the host, how diet shapes it, and the clinical problems and marketing claims a doctor will meet, each judged by its mechanism and evidence.

## Appetite and weight regulation

**Now:** Weight lost by dieting is often regained because the hormones and brain circuits that regulate body weight respond far more strongly to weight loss than to weight gain. The same signals set hunger and fullness at every meal, and understanding them is the basis for talking about weight without blame.

**Proposed:** Hunger, fullness and body weight are regulated by signals from the gut and fat tissue acting on circuits in the brain, and these signals respond far more strongly to weight loss than to weight gain. We cover how hunger and fullness are controlled, what makes a meal filling, the reward system behind eating for pleasure, and why lost weight is often regained, which is the basis for talking about weight without blame.

## Infancy & early childhood

**Now:** An infant grows faster than at any other time of life, on a diet that starts as a single fluid. The clinician's task is to fill the few nutrient gaps that milk leaves, time solids and allergens well and, often, gently correct a well-meant tradition.

**Proposed:** An infant grows faster than at any other time of life, on a diet that starts as milk alone. We cover the internal changes that set the timing of solids (falling iron stores, readiness and allergen tolerance), the feeding choices around them (breast milk, formula and responsive feeding), and the signs that call for referral.

## Childhood & adolescence

**Now:** Between the high chair and adulthood, the body lays down most of its adult bone, grows for a decade and settles habits that are just as lasting. Many of the risks at this stage come less from a missing nutrient than from the environment and from how weight is talked about, where careless comments can cause harm.

**Proposed:** Between early childhood and adulthood the body grows for a decade and lays down most of its adult bone, while eating habits form that tend to last. We cover the internal changes (growth, bone and iron), the external influences (the food environment, peers and sport), and how to recognise trouble without causing harm, since careless talk about weight can do damage at this age.

## Adulthood: working life & activity

**Now:** In the working decades, growth is finished, and the nutritional priority becomes maintenance: keeping muscle and holding weight steady while work, the hawker centre and the clock shape most meals. Midlife weight gain is usually blamed on a slowing metabolism, but size-adjusted energy expenditure barely changes in these years; the modifiable factors are activity, muscle, the food environment and a few small habits.

**Proposed:** In the working decades growth is finished and the nutritional priority becomes maintenance: keeping muscle and holding weight steady while work and the hawker centre shape most meals. We cover the internal changes (energy needs, the question of a slowing metabolism, and muscle), the external ones (activity, the food environment and alcohol), and when to refer.

## Pregnancy & lactation

**Now:** Pregnancy remodels a woman's physiology on its own, expanding her blood volume, lowering her insulin sensitivity and roughly doubling the calcium her gut absorbs. Against that automatic background, the clinician's task is to know the few nutrition decisions that matter, counsel women on them well and recognise when to refer.

**Proposed:** Pregnancy changes a woman's physiology automatically, expanding her blood volume, lowering her insulin sensitivity and roughly doubling the calcium her gut absorbs. We cover the nutritional requirements and the common problems these changes bring (nausea and gestational diabetes), the external factors (activity, food safety and screening), nutrition during breastfeeding, and when to refer.

## Menopause & midlife health

**Now:** Menopause changes a woman's body in real, specific ways, and it gets blamed for many changes it does not cause. The clinician's task is to tell the two apart, and to know which midlife problems a good diet helps and which it cannot touch.

**Proposed:** Menopause causes some specific changes in a woman's body and is blamed for others it does not cause. We cover the changes in weight, bone and cardiovascular risk and which of them menopause drives, the evidence on soy and supplements, and the midlife problems that need investigation or referral rather than a diet.

## Healthy ageing

**Now:** With age, appetite fades and the plate gets smaller, yet the need for most nutrients does not fall with it, and the need for protein rises. The advice an older patient needs is almost never "eat less": it is to eat a denser plate and, where possible, to eat it in company.

**Proposed:** With age, appetite and portion size fall, yet the need for most nutrients does not fall with them, and the need for protein rises. We cover the physiological changes and nutrient needs of older age, why older adults eat less, the effect of eating alone, and how to recognise malnutrition and when to refer.

## What a healthy diet looks like

**Now:** A patient sits down and asks the question no nutrient chart can answer: "So what should I eat?" The answer is a pattern, and it can be built from the patient's own food.

**Proposed:** Patients ask what they should eat, and the useful answer is a dietary pattern built from their own food rather than a list of nutrients. We cover the dietary guidelines and plate models, the evidence for patterns over single nutrients, and how to apply both to real patients' meals.

## Evidence vs hype, and everyday questions

**Now:** Every week a patient arrives with a headline: a superfood, a detox, a diet that "resets your metabolism." The answer is a habit of tracing the claim back to its evidence and reaching a defensible verdict, including "modest but real", in place of a list of red-flag words.

**Proposed:** Patients often bring a nutrition claim from the news or social media: a superfood, a detox, a diet said to reset the metabolism. We cover the steps of critical appraisal for tracing a claim back to its evidence and reaching a proportionate conclusion, and then apply them to everyday questions patients ask.

## Assessing diet and talking to patients

**Now:** Advice can only be as good as the assessment behind it. At first contact a doctor has to find out what a patient actually eats, read a food label with them, and say a first useful sentence without making them defensive.

**Proposed:** Advice is only as good as the assessment behind it. We cover how to find out what a patient eats (assessment methods, the diet history and reading a food label), how to interpret what the history shows, including screening for malnutrition, and how to open a brief conversation about change without doing harm.

## Behaviour-change counselling

**Now:** Knowing the right diet is rarely enough to change what a patient eats; the conversation usually matters more. A brief consult has to give that conversation a structure, draw out the patient's own motivation, turn a vague wish into one concrete goal, and do it without causing harm.

**Proposed:** Knowing the right diet is rarely enough to change what a patient eats; the conversation usually matters more. We cover why knowledge alone falls short (reward and habit), how to structure a brief consult with the 5 A's, motivational interviewing and goal setting, how to tailor it to the patient's readiness and daily life, and how to avoid harm.

## Interprofessional practice, referral and looking after yourself

**Now:** A safe clinician is defined as much by what they refer as by what they do: good advice stays within the edges of the role, and a clean hand-off follows when a patient crosses them. The clinician's own health also needs looking after.

**Proposed:** Safe practice depends on knowing where a generalist's nutrition advice ends and when to hand a patient on to a dietitian or specialist. We cover the scope of practice (when to advise and when to refer), how to make a referral and work in a team, and the clinician's own health.

## Obesity & metabolic syndrome

**Now:** A patient with a "reassuring" BMI can still be carrying the fat that matters. Obesity and the metabolic syndrome are a pattern produced by biology and environment together, so the clinical task is to read the pattern, start lifestyle-first care and know what to refer onward, without stigma.

**Proposed:** Obesity and the metabolic syndrome result from biology and environment together, and a normal BMI can still hide harmful visceral fat. We cover how weight status and the metabolic syndrome are defined, how excess fat produces the cluster of risks, lifestyle-first dietary management delivered without stigma, and when to refer.

## Type 2 diabetes

**Now:** When insulin resistance outpaces what the pancreas can compensate for, blood glucose rises, and type 2 diabetes is the point at which it stays high. Diet can prevent that progression, manage established disease and put early disease into remission, provided the change is safe with the patient's glucose-lowering drugs and the plan is made by referral.

**Proposed:** When insulin resistance outpaces what the pancreas can compensate for, blood glucose rises, and type 2 diabetes is the point at which it stays high. We cover that progression, dietary management (starting with safety alongside glucose-lowering drugs, then carbohydrate quality and dietary patterns), remission and prevention, and when to refer.

## Cardiovascular disease & hypertension

**Now:** The insulin resistance and abnormal blood lipids that run through obesity and type 2 diabetes also show up as raised blood pressure and atherosclerosis. Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in Singapore, and much of its risk is modifiable by diet: the salt in hawker soups and sauces, and the kind of fat in the cooking oil.

**Proposed:** Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in Singapore, and much of its risk can be changed by diet, notably the salt in hawker soups and sauces and the type of fat in cooking oil. We cover how diet affects the arteries and blood pressure, dietary management to lower blood pressure and protect the arteries, and when to refer.

## Chronic kidney disease

**Now:** In Singapore, chronic kidney disease is mostly the downstream damage of diabetes, and once the kidney is failing, intake has to be matched to what the kidney can still excrete. Advice that protects the public, such as eating more potassium, can then become dangerous, so the renal diet is individualised, specialist work.

**Proposed:** In Singapore, chronic kidney disease is mostly the downstream damage of diabetes, and as kidney function declines, intake has to be matched to what the kidney can still excrete. We cover what accumulates as function falls, medical nutrition therapy and why public advice such as eating more potassium can become dangerous, and when to refer.

## Undernutrition & malnutrition

**Now:** Malnutrition can be present at a normal or even high BMI, so it is rarely noticed until someone looks for it. In Singapore it hides in plain sight among frail, isolated and hospitalised older adults.

**Proposed:** Malnutrition can be present at a normal or even high BMI, so it is rarely noticed unless someone screens for it, and in Singapore it is often missed among frail, isolated and hospitalised older adults. We cover what malnutrition does to the body, how to recognise and screen for it, oral nutrition support and texture-modified food, and when to refer.

## Bone & joint health

**Now:** The ageing musculoskeleton prompts some of the most common questions a doctor hears: "What should I eat?" and "Should I take a supplement?" Bone responds to real, correctable dietary factors, while osteoarthritis and gout are almost the opposite case: their main treatment is weight loss (or, for gout, a drug), and their best-selling supplement does not work.

**Proposed:** Patients often ask what to eat and which supplements to take for their bones and joints, and the answer differs by condition. We cover bone remodelling, the dietary factors that support bone, the joints (osteoarthritis and gout, whose main treatments are weight loss and, for gout, a drug, not supplements), and when to refer.

## Brain health and dementia

**Now:** A 52-year-old whose mother has dementia asks the question doctors hear again and again: "Doctor, what should I eat so I don't get it too?" The best-supported answer is not a superfood or a supplement but control of vascular risk.

**Proposed:** Patients with a family history of dementia often ask what to eat to avoid it, and the best-supported answer is control of vascular risk rather than a particular food or supplement. We cover the pathophysiology of neurodegeneration and small-vessel disease, how diet and vascular risk factors affect dementia risk, eating difficulties in advanced dementia, and when to refer.
