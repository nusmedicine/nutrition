# Fix cohesion and signals, not attention spans

The evidence says a "dense, hard to read" textbook is best fixed by making its structure visible and its reasoning explicit, not by shortening it or breaking it into bullets. The best-supported levers are retrieval practice with explanatory feedback, which in medical trainees lifted retention at six months from 26% to 39% (d = 0.91) against restudying identical material ([Larsen et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19930508/)); placing explanation right beside the figure it explains (g = 0.63 across 58 comparisons, [Schroeder & Cenkci 2018 via TUM review](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/CHU_KR-23_Schroeder-2018_Lernmaterial.pdf)); headings and *sparing* bold (signalling g ≈ 0.53 for retention, [Schneider et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2017.11.001)); and cutting interesting-but-irrelevant material (g ≈ −0.33, [Sundararajan & Adesope 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4)). Bulleted lists help readers remember *parallel* items but can cost recall of the surrounding text ([Jansen 2014](https://benjamins.com/catalog/idj.21.2.06jan)), and teaching the causal mechanism beats feature lists and algorithms for durable diagnostic knowledge ([Baghdady et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19805783/)), so pathophysiology must stay in connected prose. The "people have low attention spans now" explanation is a myth: the 8-second goldfish statistic has no research behind it ([ScienceBlog on BBC *More or Less*](https://scienceblog.com/t-humans-eight-second-attention-span-goldfish-myth/)). The real screen problem is shallower processing and overconfidence on expository text (g = −0.32 versus paper, [Clinton 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9817.12269)), which in-text questions and deeper-processing prompts counteract. Nearly every numeric threshold in circulation (sentence length, paragraph length, items per list) comes from style guides, not experiments, and the authoring guide should label those thresholds as conventions.

## The colleague is right about density and wrong about why

The attention-span diagnosis fails on evidence and would weaken the case for revision with a sceptical colleague. The "8 seconds, less than a goldfish" figure traces to a 2015 Microsoft Canada marketing report crediting "Statistic Brain"; BBC *More or Less* found no research behind it, and psychologists told the programme that attention span is task-dependent, not a single measurable quantity ([ScienceBlog summary](https://scienceblog.com/t-humans-eight-second-attention-span-goldfish-myth/); [UW–Madison myth-busting handout](https://internet.psych.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/532-Master/532-UnitPages/Unit-09/Goldfish_MythBusting.pdf)). The BBC findings reach us second-hand, because the original page was not retrieved. What *has* been measured is screen switching: Gloria Mark's logging studies show the average time on one screen before switching fell from about 2.5 minutes in 2003–04 to **about 47 seconds** in recent years ([UCI News](https://news.uci.edu/2023/05/05/uci-podcast-if-you-cant-pay-attention-youre-not-alone)). That is a habit of interruption, not a biological ceiling. The lecture-hall version of the myth, the "10–15 minute attention limit", also collapses on inspection: a review in *Advances in Physiology Education* found "scant few primary investigations" and primary data that do not support it ([Bradbury 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/)).

The defensible concern is depth of processing on screens. Three meta-analyses agree that comprehension of expository text is modestly worse on screen than on paper: **g = −0.21** across 54 studies with no reading-time difference ([Delgado et al. 2018](https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado%202018%20dont%20throw%20away%20your%20printed%20books.pdf)), and **g = −0.32 for expository versus −0.04 for narrative** text, with screen readers also worse calibrated about their own understanding ([Clinton 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9817.12269)). Delgado et al. found the gap larger under time pressure, absent for narrative, unrelated to age, and growing over 2000–2017. Kong et al. instead found a non-significant shrinking trend ([Kong, Seo & Zhai 2018](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/comparison-of-reading-performance-on-screen-and-on-paper-a-meta-a/), DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2018.05.005), so the trend direction is unresolved. Most usefully for a web textbook, Delgado et al. report that simple measures such as writing summarising keywords or framing the reading as central rather than preliminary removed the screen deficit in primary studies. Speed is not the problem; shallow, overconfident reading is, and formatting that only speeds scanning will not fix it without prompts that force processing.

Web eyetracking explains why a wall of prose feels dense. Readers fall into the F-pattern (first lines, then the left edge) only "when there are no strong cues to attract the eyes towards meaningful information" ([Pernice 2017, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). With strong headings they switch to the layer-cake pattern, "by far the most effective way" to scan, and highly motivated readers, with "studying for a test" as NN/g's own example, fall into commitment reading, which gives the best comprehension ([Pernice 2019, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)). Medical students are commitment readers on first pass and scanners on revision, so headings carry the most leverage because they serve both modes. Readers' sense of ease does not track their performance either: in Dyson and Kipping's screen study, 55-character lines were rated easiest while 100-character lines were read faster at equal comprehension ([Dyson & Kipping 1998](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/5671)). "Hard to read" can therefore be a perception of visual density (long paragraphs, little white space) as much as a comprehension failure, though perception still matters for whether students open the book at all.

Student behaviour is what raises the stakes. In one Melbourne first-year cohort, platform logs showed each lecture-notes file accessed **167 times** on average against **31** for recommended online textbook chapters ([Judd & Elliott 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28970187/)). Self-report elsewhere puts textbooks among the most-used resources for *new* material ([Wynter et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30683084/)). Michigan students read a 700-page exam review book **2.1 times** and completed about 3,600 practice MCQs during Step 1 preparation ([Burk-Rafel et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29065026/)), which shows that length is not the barrier when a text is seen as high-yield and aligned with assessment. In a Saudi survey, one-third of students said limited English stopped them understanding textbooks ([Jameel et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30654842/)), a reminder that linguistic density is a separate barrier for bilingual readers, which matters in Singapore. Cognitive load theory, as applied to health-professions education, names the underlying problem: medical content has high *element interactivity*, many ideas that must be held and integrated at once, which overwhelms novice working memory when presented all together ([Young et al. 2014, AMEE Guide 86](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24593808/), PMID 24593808). "Dense" most plausibly means too many new interacting elements per paragraph, links left implicit, and weak cues to structure. Each of those can be fixed without removing content.

## Ten principles, ranked by the strength of their evidence

Strength is graded here as **strong** (several meta-analyses, plus replication in health-professions learners), **moderate** (meta-analytic but mostly short lab studies, or a consistent experimental programme), **limited** (few or small studies, or narrow populations) and **convention** (style-guide rule with no cited study). One caveat applies across the board. Most of this literature uses 5–20-minute lessons, immediate tests and psychology undergraduates, and a 2025 meta-analysis of Mayer's own multimedia corpus found a pooled **g = 0.37 that is declining year on year**, well below the "median d" figures in Mayer's handbook tables ([Cromley & Chen 2025](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927), DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2025.100730). Directions are robust; magnitudes for weeks-long self-paced textbook study are smaller than the headline numbers.

Three further strands support the table without earning rows of their own. Segmenting a lesson into learner-paced units gives small-to-medium gains and lowers load ([Rey et al. 2019](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4), DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4), but the evidence is almost all from system-paced animations; static text is already self-paced, so its value for a book lies mainly in structuring. Supplied concept maps beat text or outlines only modestly, and the big gains come when learners *build* maps ([Nesbit & Adesope 2006](https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/research/nesbitadesope2006.htm); the subgroup figures of roughly g = 0.82 constructing versus 0.37 studying are unverified). Self-explanation prompts add to testing in Year 1 medical students, with testing plus explanation at 40% versus study alone at 20% after six months ([Larsen et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23746156/), PMID 23746156), and they help most on unfamiliar topics ([Chamberland et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21649701/), PMID 21649701). A school-mathematics worked-example meta-analysis found the opposite moderator for self-explanation ([Barbieri et al. 2023](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2024/1/25-1)), so this conflict is unresolved outside medicine.

The multimedia principles do transfer to medical students. Redesigning a bullet-heavy surgery lecture on shock to Mayer's principles produced delayed-transfer gains of **d = 0.83 at one week and d = 1.17 at four weeks** in Year 3 students ([Issa et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23488758/), PMID 23488758). The comparison condition, text-heavy bullet slides, is close to the "dense" problem, but the design was non-randomised and single-site, and it tested slides rather than chapters. The broader online-learning meta-analysis makes the same point from the other side: internet-based instruction barely differs from other formats (knowledge ES 0.12), so design, not medium, drives learning, with practice exercises (0.40) and feedback (0.68, from only 2 studies) among the strongest design variables ([Cook et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18780847/), PMID 18780847; [Cook et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20520049/), PMID 20520049).

Several figures in circulation need flags before they enter the authoring guide. The Schneider et al. effect sizes and moderators come from a secondary German review that read the paper in full, not from the paper itself. Adesope et al.'s overall g = 0.61 is inferred from a range in a secondary source, while the classroom g = 0.67 is better attested. Rowland's g = 0.50 for testing versus restudy is reported via [Greving & Richter 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/full) (DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412). Ginns's 2006 contiguity estimate is reported as both g = 0.72 and d ≈ 0.85–0.89 by different secondary sources. Rey's 2012 seductive-detail range (recall g −0.27 to −0.95) is inconsistent across summaries, and a 2026 AERA multilevel meta-analysis reporting g = −0.16 across 177 effects is a non-peer-reviewed conference abstract ([AERA 2026](https://convention2.allacademic.com/one/aera/aera26/online_program_direct_link/view_paper/2273554/)). The two signalling meta-analyses also disagree on prior knowledge: Schneider et al. found high-knowledge learners benefit *more* from text cues, while Richter et al. found text–picture signals help mainly *low*-knowledge learners ([Richter et al. 2016](https://publikationen.uni-tuebingen.de/xmlui/handle/10900/74617?show=full)). Testing-format evidence conflicts too: Rowland found short-answer beat MCQ, Adesope et al. found the reverse, and both found matching practice to the final test format helps.

## Choose the device by the relation between the ideas

The unifying rule across the evidence is to pick the form by the logical relation among the units of content, because informationally equivalent representations differ in how much work the reader must do to extract a relation ([Larkin & Simon 1987](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/F12/cs200/Readings/larkin.whyadiagramissometimesworth.1987.pdf)). Prose is the only form that carries *because*, *therefore*, *unless* and *only if*; lists carry membership; tables carry comparison; diagrams carry position, sequence and connection. The house style already has most of these devices (chapter maps, trees, spectra, flows, example boxes, the 🔬🍜🍳 callouts, tables); the gain lies in governing them by relation type and keeping each one scarce.

| Content relation | Device | Rule and threshold | Status of the threshold |
|---|---|---|---|
| Causal chain, argument or caveat ("A raises B because C, unless D") | **Prose** with topic sentence, given-new order and explicit connectives | Never bullet a causal chain; if it has **4 or more steps** that readers must retrace, *add* a flow beside the prose, not in its place | Rule rests on case analysis ([Tufte](https://www.literature.wolkersdorfer.info/literature/The%20Cognitive%20Style%20of%20PowerPoint%20Pitching%20Out%20Corrupts%20Within.pdf)) and medical-education RCTs ([Baghdady et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19805783/)); the 4-step trigger is a heuristic |
| Unordered set of parallel, homogeneous items (food sources, red flags) | **Bulleted list** introduced by a full sentence saying what unites the items and whether the list is exhaustive | **2–7 items** ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/lists)); **≤6** short items of similar length ([NHS](https://service-manual.nhs.uk/content/formatting)); never one item ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/lists)); items grammatically parallel | Convention. The empirical support is only that bullets aid recall of homogeneous items ([Jansen 2014](https://benjamins.com/catalog/idj.21.2.06jan)) |
| Two or three short parallel items | **In-sentence enumeration** ("Three factors drive X: A, B and C") | Keeps the enumeration signal while keeping the reasoning in prose | Heuristic, consistent with signalling research ([Lorch & Lorch 1996](https://scholars.uky.edu/es/publications/effects-of-organizational-signals-on-free-recall-of-expository-te/)) |
| Strict sequence or priority (stages, a counselling protocol) | **Numbered list**; the house `.flow` for 4–7 stages; a branching flowchart if there are decision points | Number only when order matters ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/scannable-content/lists); [Google](https://developers.google.com/style/lists)) | Convention; the house 4–7 range is a heuristic |
| Several items × several shared attributes (vitamins × function, sources, deficiency) | **Table** | Use when items have **multiple properties** ([Google](https://developers.google.com/style/lists)); with only term + definition, prefer a term list; order rows by mechanism, severity or category, alphabetical only for look-up; add one sentence stating the relation the table exists to show | "Multiple properties" is convention; meaningful ordering is evidence ([Kauffman & Kiewra 2010](https://kiewra.unl.edu/sites/unl.edu.cehs.kiewra/files/media/file/What%20Makes%20a%20Matrix%20So%20Effective.pdf)); "3+ attributes" is a heuristic |
| Classification with real subdivisions | **Tree** (`.concept-tree`) | House cap of 3 levels; nested lists at most 2 levels | Heuristic; NN/g finds disclosure beyond 2 levels "typically" loses users ([Nielsen 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)) |
| Many-to-many relations (one nutrient acting on several systems) | **Concept map** with labelled links, paired with prose | Use only for genuinely networked content; better still as a learner task ("sketch the links between…") | Moderate evidence for constructed maps, small for supplied ones ([Nesbit & Adesope 2006](https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/research/nesbitadesope2006.htm)) |
| Continuum (evidence strength, glycaemic response) | **Spectrum** | Use wherever categories are ends of a range, to avoid a false either/or | House convention, no direct evidence |
| Mechanism with spatial or pathway structure | **Labelled figure** adjacent to its interpreting paragraph | Prose *interprets* the figure rather than re-narrating every arrow; caption explains; no "see Figure 3" two sections back | Strong evidence ([Schroeder & Cenkci 2018](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/CHU_KR-23_Schroeder-2018_Lernmaterial.pdf)) |
| Content needed for, or directly applying, the adjacent objective (clinical link, worked example, practice translation) | **Callout / example box**, of a consistent named type | Earns its place only by serving the objective; trivia and "did you know" asides are cut or moved out of the flow | Inference from coherence and signalling evidence; no direct study of boxes |
| Genuinely optional depth, prerequisite refreshers, self-test answers | **Collapsible callout** (`collapse="true"`) with an informative title | Never core teaching text; no nesting; title names the content, not "More detail"; expect most readers not to open it; check print/PDF expansion | NN/g usability guidance ([Wang 2023](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accordions-on-desktop/)); no learning-outcome study exists |
| Short gloss, citation or aside tied to one sentence | **Margin note / sidenote** | One or two lines; on narrow screens it collapses behind a toggle or inline, so nothing essential goes there | Design argument only ([Tufte CSS](https://edwardtufte.github.io/tufte-css/)) |

The common pattern in good expository chapters is a three-beat mix: an explanatory paragraph that carries the *why*, a table or figure that carries the *what* and *which*, and one sentence that draws the take-home relation out of the device. Brick et al.'s fact-box trial shows why the third beat matters: tables improved comprehension while in view (79.6% vs 69.7% correct) but barely improved recall six weeks later (29.8% vs 27.7%) ([Brick et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7137953/)). Kauffman and Kiewra found that extracting key ideas from text, by itself, did not help; the matrix worked because it put related facts next to each other ([Kauffman & Kiewra 2010](https://kiewra.unl.edu/sites/unl.edu.cehs.kiewra/files/media/file/What%20Makes%20a%20Matrix%20So%20Effective.pdf)). Shortening text is not the mechanism; localising related information is.

### Sentence, paragraph and page thresholds are conventions, not findings

No meta-analysis or strong experiment establishes an optimal sentence or paragraph length for adult expository text, and the often-repeated "American Press Institute" sentence-length comprehension figures could not be traced to a primary source. The guides still converge, and their numbers make reasonable lint thresholds if the guide labels them as such.

| Parameter | Threshold to adopt | Sources | Status |
|---|---|---|---|

Plain-language guidance transfers to medical students in its structure and needs adapting in its vocabulary. ISO 24495-1:2023 defines plain language relative to "intended readers" and explicitly covers technical writing ([PLAIN on ISO 24495-1](https://plainlanguage.com/what-is-plain-language/iso-plain-language-standard/)), which makes it the right frame, rather than NHS reading-age targets set for stressed members of the public. The rules to adopt wholesale are front-loading at every level, descriptive headings, topic sentences, active voice and avoiding nominalisations ("conduct an analysis of" becomes "analyse"). The rule to invert is jargon avoidance: for students the term *is* the content, so the house pattern of bold term plus a plain one-line gloss at first use ("**haem iron**, the iron in meat") is the correct adaptation of GOV.UK's "explain on first use". GOV.UK's claim that specialists prefer plain English even more strongly (80% preferring clear English in legal documents) is not attributed to a named study on its page, so the guide should cite it only as GOV.UK's position.

### Fitting the evidence to the book's existing furniture

Several current house rules already match the evidence, and a few need tightening. The **learning objectives** behave like pre-questions, which help the content they name (g = 0.54) and do nothing for the rest (g = 0.04) ([St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)), so the 4–6 objectives must name the chapter's core mechanisms and decisions, never peripheral facts. The **chapter map and "at a glance" overview** work as the advance organiser; expect a small benefit, strongest when the frame links new content to an earlier chapter's idea by name rather than listing contents. **Headings outperform preview sentences** at directing attention to topic structure ([Lorch, Lemarié & Chen 2013](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/signaling-topic-structure-via-headings-or-preview-sentences/), DOI 10.1016/S1135-755X(13)70011-3), which supports the existing ban on narrated roadmaps. It does not contradict NN/g's inverted-pyramid advice to state the bottom line first ([Schade 2018, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/inverted-pyramid/)): a movement's opening sentence that states a *finding* is content, while one that announces what follows is metacommentary. The **example box** rules (anchor examples, one per idea, one sentence of clinical detail) are a direct implementation of the coherence principle. The **"no section summaries"** rule is right on the evidence, for reasons given below. The main gap is placement of retrieval: the quiz sits only at the chapter's end, whereas the medical evidence favours questions embedded in or immediately after each section, with elaborated feedback. Web modules with embedded case-based self-assessment questions scored 78.9% vs 76.2%, and 71% of residents rated them more efficient despite taking longer ([Cook et al. 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16501263/), PMID 16501263).

## Five failure modes undo well-meant restructuring

### Lists fragment causal reasoning

The strongest risk in responding to a "too dense" complaint is converting mechanism paragraphs into bullets. Jansen's five studies found bulleting improved recall of the bulleted items, less so when items were heterogeneous, and in two studies *reduced* recall of the surrounding text ([Jansen 2014](https://benjamins.com/catalog/idj.21.2.06jan)). The best-known case analysis is the Columbia shuttle debris slide, where a reassuring title was undermined by a fourth-level bullet stating that the debris was roughly 640 times larger than the test data; Tufte argues that "as analysis becomes more causal, multivariate, comparative, evidence-based … the more damaging the bullet list becomes" ([Tufte, *The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint*](https://www.literature.wolkersdorfer.info/literature/The%20Cognitive%20Style%20of%20PowerPoint%20Pitching%20Out%20Corrupts%20Within.pdf)). The Columbia Accident Investigation Board said a senior manager could read the slide "and not realize that it addresses a life-threatening situation" ([CAIB via Tufte](https://www.edwardtufte.com/notebook/columbia-accident-investigation-board-the-boeing-powerpoint-slide/)). This is expert and case evidence, not experiment, and Doumont argues the fault lies in how slides are used rather than in lists themselves ([Doumont 2005](https://www.bibsonomy.org/bibtex/7b68fcf9924d2c1eccc8eea12b70b46c)). The medical-education experiments point the same way, though: causal explanation beats feature lists for durable diagnostic knowledge ([Woods 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18045369/)). The house rule that "three or more parallel points" become a list is sound only if *parallel* is read strictly. Three consequences that each follow from the one before are a chain, not a set, and belong in a paragraph. No peer-reviewed experiment compares bullets with paragraphs for causal science content on transfer measures, so this remains the key missing study.

### Over-signalling cancels the signal

Cues work by redirecting attention, so they work only while they are scarce. When Lorch and colleagues underlined the target sentences *plus* half the non-target sentences, cued recall fell back to the no-cue level ([Lorch, Lorch & Klusewitz 1995](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/effects-of-typographical-cues-on-reading-and-recall-of-text/)), and when only half the topics in a text were signalled, the unsignalled content suffered ([Lorch & Lorch 1996](https://scholars.uky.edu/es/publications/effects-of-organizational-signals-on-free-recall-of-expository-te/)). The NHS states the practical version: "too much bold makes it hard for readers to tell which content matters most" ([NHS formatting](https://service-manual.nhs.uk/content/formatting)). Three versions of this failure threaten the book. First, bold run-in lead-ins on every paragraph turn prose into a disguised list; they suit the five-beat anatomy, where paragraphs really are parallel entries, but not a continuous argument. Second, a paragraph that bolds five newly defined terms is a density alarm rather than a formatting choice: the fix is to pre-teach some of those terms earlier, not to bold them all. Third, callout inflation, with a 🔬🍜🍳 triad plus a clinical pearl plus an example box on one screen, dilutes the cue value of each box. Consistent callout types act as organisational signals, and their value falls as their number rises. Signalling also does nothing for simple structures ([Lorch & Lorch 1996](https://scholars.uky.edu/es/publications/effects-of-organizational-signals-on-free-recall-of-expository-te/)), so a short, linear section needs no extra headings.

### Help for novices becomes redundancy for experts

Support that rescues novices can slow or harm learners who already hold the schema, the **expertise-reversal effect** ([Kalyuga et al. 2003](https://ro.uow.edu.au/edupapers/136); [Kalyuga 2007](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-007-9054-3)). The text-level version is striking: high-knowledge readers learned more deeply from a *less* cohesive text that forced gap-filling inferences, while low-knowledge readers needed the explicit version ([McNamara et al. 1996 summary](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing)). Leppink and van den Heuvel warn explicitly against the misconception that "lower cognitive load [is] always … beneficial to learning" ([Leppink & van den Heuvel 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26016429/), PMID 26016429). For this book, the readers are novices in nutrition biochemistry but not in all of physiology, so explicit cohesion is the right default for new mechanisms, while prerequisite re-teaching belongs in a one-line gloss or a collapsible refresher that stronger students can skip. Redundancy also covers figures: when a labelled pathway owns an idea, prose that re-narrates every arrow makes the reader process the same content twice, and integration helps most when text and picture *complement* each other (g = 0.74) rather than when the picture is self-explanatory (g = 0.26, non-significant) ([Schroeder & Cenkci via TUM](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/CHU_KR-23_Schroeder-2018_Lernmaterial.pdf)). This vindicates the project's existing rule against tabulating what a figure already owns.

### Hidden content is unread content

Collapsibles look like a tidy answer to density, but NN/g reports that "valuable content that is hidden under an accordion may be missed altogether", that accordions are wrong where "users are meant to read continuously", and that they print poorly unless expanded ([Wang 2023, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accordions-on-desktop/)). Readers "treat clicks like currency", and "relevance trumps page length": people scroll through valuable, scannable content ([Loranger 2014, NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/accordions-complex-content/)). Because "anything shown first is implicitly important" ([Nielsen 2006](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)), what stays visible tells students what is examinable, and anything collapsed reads as optional. GOV.UK applies the same logic to footnotes: if it matters, put it in the body; otherwise delete it ([GOV.UK structure](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-structure/)). No controlled study shows whether collapsing optional detail improves learning from text; the rationale for collapsing refreshers is an inference from expertise reversal. Collapse, therefore, only what most readers can skip without missing an objective, and use collapse freely for quiz answers, where hiding the answer *is* the point.

### Summaries feel useful; retrieval is useful

Students prefer summaries and rereading because they feel fluent, and the evidence explains the illusion: restudy wins on a five-minute test, testing wins at two days and one week, and restudying inflates confidence ([Roediger & Karpicke 2006](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/test-enhanced-learning-taking-memory-tests-improves-long-term-ret/)). Dunlosky et al. rate practice testing and distributed practice as high utility, and rereading, highlighting and student-written summaries as low ([Dunlosky et al. 2013 via APS](https://psychologicalscience.org/?p=82014)). No study compares author-written end-of-section summaries with end-of-section questions directly, so the strongest defensible rule is to convert key-point boxes into "check yourself" prompts whose answer, revealed after an attempt, *is* the summary. Kerfoot's validated format (a short case, an MCQ, then the answer, a teaching-point summary and an explanation) produced d = 1.01 for students six to eight months after a rotation ([Kerfoot et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17209889/), PMID 17209889). Feedback must explain: in the Kopp trials, erroneous examples with bare right/wrong feedback impaired learning ([Kopp et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19930513/)), which supports the house rule that wrong-answer feedback names the misconception and gives the mechanism. Screen readers' overconfidence ([Clinton 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9817.12269)) is a further reason to embed these checks, since a wrong answer is the cheapest correction for an illusion of understanding.

### Two traps from the plain-language and microlearning camps

Two popular prescriptions do not transfer. NN/g's "use half the word count" came from promotional travel copy ([Nielsen 1997](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/)), and the Morkes and Nielsen gains (scannable +47%, concise +58%, combined +124%) came from about 10 users per condition on a seven-page tourism site, measured on fact-finding tasks with a composite score driven by error rates ([Morkes & Nielsen 1997](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/)). The transferable principle is to cut filler, hedging and redundancy, not explanation. Microlearning, likewise, is well liked but evidenced mainly at the level of learner reactions: in a scoping review of 17 health-professions studies, 94% measured reactions and none reached patient outcomes ([De Gagne et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31339105/), PMID 31339105). Short, spaced units work as reinforcement after teaching, not as a substitute for integrated explanation. Seductive-detail worries need the opposite caution. Singapore context that carries the practical point (a hawker swap, a festival fast) serves the objective and is not a seductive detail; trivia, historical asides and decorative images are. Novices are hurt most by seductive details, and the harm is larger under time limits ([Sundararajan & Adesope 2020 via WSU](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning)), which describes students cramming before an exam.

## Revise a dense section in five passes

The evidence implies an order of operations for revising a dense section. Cohesion and signalling come before any change of format, because many "dense" paragraphs turn out to be one idea badly sequenced rather than a list in disguise.

| Pass | What to do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Objective test | For every sentence, box and image, ask whether it serves this section's objective; cut or move what does not | Coherence evidence ([Sundararajan & Adesope 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09522-4)) |
| 2. Pre-teach and gloss | Move definitions of the key players *before* the mechanism paragraph; count new terms per paragraph and redistribute if the count exceeds a handful | Pre-training; working-memory limit ([Cowan 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/)) |
| 3. Cohesion rewrite | Topic sentence stating the claim; each sentence opening with given information and ending on the new term; explicit *because*, *so*, *however*, *only when*; repeat the key noun rather than "this" or "it" | Given-new processing ([Haviland & Clark 1974](https://web.stanford.edu/~clark/1970s/)); stress position ([Gopen & Swan 1990](https://acawiki.org/The_Science_of_Scientific_Writing)); explicit connectives in L1 and L2 ([Degand & Sanders 2002](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/the-impact-of-relational-markers-on-expository-text-comprehension/)) |
| 4. Re-form by relation | Only now ask whether the rewritten content is a set, a sequence, a comparison, a hierarchy or a network, and apply the device table; keep the *why* in prose next to the device | Relation-matched representation ([Larkin & Simon 1987](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/F12/cs200/Readings/larkin.whyadiagramissometimesworth.1987.pdf)); matrices ([Kauffman & Kiewra 2010](https://kiewra.unl.edu/sites/unl.edu.cehs.kiewra/files/media/file/What%20Makes%20a%20Matrix%20So%20Effective.pdf)) |
| 5. Signal and check | Descriptive heading at each real shift; bold only defining uses; place figures beside their paragraph; end the movement with one retrieval or "explain why" prompt with explanatory feedback | Signalling, contiguity and testing evidence (table above) |

Pass 3 alone often resolves the complaint. Tufte observes that Feynman's 1,800-page physics lectures use no bullets and only two levels of hierarchy ([Tufte](https://www.literature.wolkersdorfer.info/literature/The%20Cognitive%20Style%20of%20PowerPoint%20Pitching%20Out%20Corrupts%20Within.pdf)), which shows that well-built sentences can carry difficult material on their own. Readability formulas cannot judge the result, because they count word and sentence length while ignoring cohesion, layout and the reader's knowledge; Redish recommends testing with real readers instead ([Redish 2000](https://ftp.math.utah.edu/pub/tex/bib/idx/jcd/24/3/132_137.html)). Cohesion indices of the Coh-Metrix kind (referential overlap, connective density) sit closer to the real problem ([Graesser et al. 2004](https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/facpubs/17131)), but the cheapest valid test remains watching two or three students read a revised section and asking them to explain the mechanism back.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the colleague's complaint from a problem of *amount* to a problem of *visibility*: the book's content is probably not too much, but its structure and its links are not visible enough on the page, and the reader is given too few prompts to process it deeply. That changes what revision means. It is mostly additive for structure (headings, topic sentences, connectives, adjacent figures, embedded questions) and subtractive only for material that fails the objective test or duplicates a figure. The most valuable change the evidence supports, and the one the book does not yet make, is moving retrieval from the chapter's end into each movement, with explanatory feedback, because that is the one intervention with large, durable effects in medical learners *and* a direct remedy for overconfident screen reading.

Two cautions should shape the authoring-guide section. First, the guide should separate evidence from convention on the page itself, because the numbers that are easiest to lint (25 words, 150 words, 7 items) are the least evidenced, while the best-evidenced rules (cue sparingly, keep causal chains in prose, put figure and explanation together, test rather than summarise) resist automatic checking and depend on editorial judgement. Second, expertise reversal means no single register suits every reader, so the book's layering (core prose visible, refreshers and depth collapsible, glosses in one line) is a principled response to the evidence rather than a compromise. The decisive experiment, bullets versus cohesive prose for causal science text measured on transfer, has not been run, and the book's own in-chapter quizzes would let the author run a small version of it.
