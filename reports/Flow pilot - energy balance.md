## 2

**Before**

> Most of the energy the body spends goes on keeping it alive at rest. **Basal metabolic rate (BMR)** is the energy needed to keep the body running at complete rest (heartbeat, breathing, kidneys and the constant turnover of cells), and at typically 60–70% of TEE it is the largest component. BMR is driven mainly by **fat-free (lean) mass**, the body mass other than fat, because its muscle and organs are metabolically active while stored fat is much less so. BMR falls with age, partly as lean mass is lost; it is lower when thyroid hormone is low; and it differs between the sexes largely because body composition does [cite].

## 7

**Before**

> BMI cannot tell muscle from fat, so it misclassifies the very muscular and can miss excess fat in people who are slim by weight. BMI is therefore a screening tool, and its cut-offs depend on the population: Asian cut-offs for overweight are set lower than the international ones, because the risk of diabetes and cardiovascular disease rises at a lower BMI in Asian populations [cite] (see the *Obesity & metabolic syndrome* chapter). Waist circumference adds information that BMI does not give. It captures **central (visceral) fat**, the fat stored around the abdomen, which carries more metabolic risk than fat on the hips and thighs [cite]. Energy expenditure itself is measured with research tools. BMR is measured by **calorimetry**, directly from the heat the body produces or indirectly from the oxygen it consumes and the carbon dioxide it produces. Total expenditure in free-living people (those going about their normal lives) is measured with **doubly labelled water**, a harmless isotope tracer followed over one to two weeks; it is the gold standard behind the expenditure shares given earlier [cite]. In the clinic, the estimating equations are used instead.

## 9

**Before**

> Energy balance is actively regulated by two physiological responses that oppose changes in body weight: a fall in energy expenditure after weight loss, and signalling of the size of the fat stores to the brain (see the *Appetite and weight regulation* chapter). When someone loses weight, "energy out" falls by more than their smaller body should require. This extra drop, **adaptive thermogenesis** (also called metabolic adaptation), makes lost weight easy to regain. Appetite adds to the effect: hunger rises after weight loss and is still raised a year later [cite]. Adipose tissue also signals to the brain how much fat is stored, by releasing a hormone, **leptin**, in rough proportion to the amount of fat carried [cite]. The brain uses leptin as a long-term signal of fat stores, often likened to a fuel gauge, to regulate appetite and body weight over months and years. If leptin signals a large store, extra leptin might be expected to cut appetite and weight. Simply giving it does not, however, reverse common obesity [cite]; the reason is taken up under leptin in the *Appetite and weight regulation* chapter.

**Before**

> The rule "calories in minus calories out" is correct accounting but a poor basis for weight management, because the "out" column itself shifts in response to intake. The equation holds: energy that was never eaten cannot be stored, and a sustained deficit does lower weight. The two sides are linked, however: when intake falls, energy expenditure falls too, and because body fat releases leptin in rough proportion to its size, a shrinking store sends a weaker signal to the brain, which uses leptin to regulate body weight (see the *Appetite and weight regulation* chapter).

> Singapore's food environment tends to favour a high energy intake.
