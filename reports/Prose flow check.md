# Prose flow check (2026-10-25)

> **Status (2026-10-25, later the same day):** all flags below have been acted on. The structural pass (`728dc8d`) added orienting sentences under bare `##` headings, rewrote the closing answers, merged orphan safety paragraphs and moved cross-reference asides; the cohesion pass then rewrote or skipped (with reasons) every remaining flag, including the logic slips, and six adversarial verifiers checked every hunk. Definition lists and the Macronutrients five-beat labels were kept as house style. Line numbers below refer to the text before those passes.

This check was read-only: no chapter was edited. It covers the 29 prose chapters in `book/chapters/`. Two passes were made:

1. **A script.** It scored every prose paragraph for sentence length, runs of short sentences and linking openers.
2. **Seven readers.** Between them they read every chapter in full. They looked for seven patterns, listed below, and drafted rewrites for the worst passages.

Every quoted passage was checked against the current files.

## The short answer

The book is not choppy in the usual sense:

- Average sentence length is 17–21 words in every content chapter.
- Only 12–27% of sentences have nine words or fewer.
- The script flagged just six of 681 paragraphs on length alone.

The roughness you noticed is a problem of **cohesion**. Many paragraphs set correct statements side by side without saying how they relate (cause, contrast, example, consequence). That leaves the reader to supply the "because", "so" or "but".

This is probably partly a by-product of the four phrase-level sweeps. Each of them rewrote single sentences for plainness, so every sentence is now correct on its own. The links between them were never the target.

The readers flagged **279 passages**, of which **107 are major**. Verdict by chapter:

|---|---|
| Uneven | 5: Macronutrients, Micronutrients & hydration, Gut microbiome, Type 2 diabetes, Bone health |
| Mostly smooth | 21 |
| Smooth | 3: Part II intro and the two case chapters |

No chapter is choppy throughout.

## What the patterns are

| Code | Pattern | Count | What the reader has to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| JUMP | Topic shift inside a paragraph, or between paragraphs, with no transition | 80 | Work out why the next sentence is here |
| STACK | Three or more declaratives side by side, no stated relation | 63 | Supply "because", "so", "but", "for example" |
| SIGNPOST | Section with no orienting sentence, or a paragraph whose point comes last | 33 | Read on to learn what the section is for |
| LISTIFY | An argument chopped into bullets or a glossary, or a bare list lead-in | 27 | Rebuild the chain the bullets broke |
| ASIDE | A parenthetical or cross-reference sentence dropped between two linked sentences | 27 | Hold the thread across an interruption |
| REPEAT | The same sentence frame in consecutive sentences ("A referral… A referral… A referral…") | 25 | Notice the rhythm instead of the content |
| SHORT | A run of very short sentences, or a staccato or verbless opener | 24 | Join fragments that belong together |

## Book-wide patterns worth fixing as a pass, not one by one

**1. Stacked facts: the main cause of choppiness.**
The clearest cases are the Micronutrients vitamin A, D and K paragraphs, the calcium-sources paragraph, the regulatory paragraphs in Dietary assessment, and most of the Singapore context sections. Example (Micronutrients, L115):

> Vitamin K is needed for blood clotting. Its best-known deficiency is in newborns… Adults get vitamin K from leafy greens and fermented foods.

The rule is already in AUTHOR §11j pass 3, "Cohesion rewrite": a topic sentence that states the claim, each sentence starting from what the reader already has, and explicit links. It was simply never applied as a separate pass. A cohesion pass is the single change that would do most for the flow.

**2. Singapore context paragraphs that list unrelated facts.**
There are 28 flags across 19 chapters: three local facts in one paragraph with no thread between them. Examples are CKD L316 (War on Diabetes, then the AHA's CKM concept, then ACE's referral stance), Brain health L295, Behaviour change, Undernutrition, Energy balance and Childhood L390 (activity and eating disorders in one paragraph). The fix is one topic sentence per paragraph. Where two facts don't share a point, split them.

**3. `##` headings with no orienting sentence.**
46 `##` headings go straight into a `###`, a figure, a callout or an island. The most affected are Gut microbiome (4), Micronutrients, Dietary assessment and Digestion (3 each). Many of these come from the Part IV fixed scaffold (Pathophysiology → Dietary management → When to refer). AUTHOR has no rule here. A one-line rule would fix it as a pass: "every `##` opens with one sentence saying what the movement covers".

**4. "N terms describe X:" glossaries.**
These are **house style, not a fault.** AUTHOR §11j pass 2 asks for a definition list *before* the mechanism. The readers flagged them anyway, and the real problems are narrower:

- **Re-narration.** The paragraph after the list retells the same chain, so each term is met twice. Examples: Digestion L233 (fat absorption), Gut microbiome L143 (fermentation), Childhood L211 (REDs).
- **Misplacement.** The list sits *inside* a causal chain instead of before it. Examples: Menopause L114 (oestrogen → RANKL → resorption), Obesity L140, Bone L390 (case-finding sequence).
- **Mismatch with the lead-in.** Macronutrients L280 says "three features", then lists five terms that don't match the three.
- **Two in a row.** Integrative metabolism L85.

The fix keeps the lists: put each before its paragraph, and have the paragraph *use* the terms instead of redefining them. Some readers proposed folding the lists into prose. That conflicts with §11j, so I have not counted it as a recommendation.

**5. Bold run-in labels.**
The Macronutrients five-beat (**What it is. / What it does. / From plate to cell. / Quality**) is mandated by AUTHOR §11a. The readers' point is that each beat starts cold. Within the rule, the fix is to keep the label and make the first sentence after it link back to the beat before. Elsewhere, §11j "Signals, kept scarce" already forbids run-in labels on the paragraphs of an argument, yet they appear there:

| Chapter | Labels |
|---|---|
| Integrative metabolism | 9 |
| Childhood | 8 |
| Obesity | 6 |
| Adulthood | 5 |
| Dietary guidelines | 4 |

Those are breaches of an existing rule. In Dietary guidelines they open the Mediterranean and DASH subsections (**What it is. / The evidence, with its caveats.**).

**6. The "No, and…" close.**
All four Part II life-stage chapters answer their closing question with the same formula:

- Childhood L354: "No, and getting that wrong can cause harm."
- Adulthood L279: "No, and believing it leads to the wrong remedies."
- Menopause L303: "No, and promising one does a disservice…"
- Healthy ageing L255: "No, and that misreading does real harm."

Appetite (L420, "No.") and Behaviour change (L254, "Not by the patient knowing more…") do the same. The formula comes from the `<close question>` scaffold. A full-sentence answer that names the misconception would read better, for example: "Metabolism does not slow appreciably through these decades; believing it does leads to the wrong remedies."

**7. One-sentence orphan paragraphs, mostly in safety material.**
These are single sentences left as their own paragraph:

- Type 2 diabetes L135–140 (carbohydrate consistency on insulin; the 15-15 rule) and L316 (Ramadan review).
- CVD L141–146 (end of the sodium section, ending on "It is not safe for everyone.").
- Obesity L104.

**8. Mid-paragraph asides.**
These are whole parenthetical cross-reference sentences between two linked sentences, for example Micronutrients L215 ("(For where in the gut iron is absorbed, see…)"). Cross-references are wanted. They read better at the end of the paragraph.

## Suggested way to fix it (if you want it)

1. **Two quick structural passes.** These are mechanical and low-risk:
   - an orienting sentence under each bare `##`;
   - the "No, and…" closes;
   - the orphan safety paragraphs;
   - moving mid-paragraph cross-reference asides to paragraph ends.
2. **A cohesion pass, chapter by chapter.** Start with the five uneven chapters, then the Singapore context sections across the book. This needs judgement. Use the readers' drafts below as starting points, then run the usual adversarial verification for meaning and hedges.
3. **Fix the logic slips above.** These are your call where the facts are concerned.
4. **Optionally, add rules to AUTHOR.md.** Candidates are §11j (one topic sentence per Singapore context paragraph; every `##` opens with a sentence) and a `copy-audit` rule for bare `##` → `###` and for "^No, and".

### digestion-absorption.qmd

- **L80 · STACK** (Digestion: motility, enzymes and hormones): “**Gastric emptying**, the passage of food from the stomach into the duodenum, is actively regulated. A meal leaves the stomach over a few hours.”  
  *Problem:* The timing fact separates 'actively regulated' from the example that shows the regulation. The migrating motor complex, a between-meals topic, is then added to the same paragraph (no blank line) with no transition (also a JUMP).  
  *Draft:* **Gastric emptying**, the passage of food from the stomach into the duodenum, takes a few hours and is actively regulated: signals from the duodenum slow it, so, counter-intuitively, a fatty or large meal empties more slowly, which is partly why a greasy meal "sits heavy".  The gut keeps moving between meals too. A cyclical "housekeeper" wave, the **migrating motor complex**, sweeps undigested residue and bacteria onward roughly every 1–2 hours; the hormone motilin triggers it [@deloose2012mmc].
- **L116 · JUMP** (Digestion: motility, enzymes and hormones): “Salivary **amylase**, which starts the digestion of starch, stops working once it meets the stomach's acid. In the duodenum, the pancreas adds bicarbonate”  
  *Problem:* After the table, the prose describes the stomach (L111-114), goes back to the mouth, then on to the duodenum. The last two sentences are a short stack ('...which neutralises that acid. Neutralisation gives...'). The rewrite covers L111-118 in mouth -> stomach -> duodenum order.  
  *Draft:* Salivary **amylase** starts the digestion of starch in the mouth but stops working once it meets the stomach's acid. In the stomach, **parietal cells** make that hydrochloric acid, which denatures protein and kills microbes, and also **intrinsic factor**, which is needed much further along the small intestine to absorb vitamin B12; **chief cells** make **pepsin**, the enzyme that cuts protein into peptides [@statpearlsStomach]. When the acid contents reach the duodenum, the pancreas adds bicarbonate to neutralise them, giving the pancreatic enzymes the near-neutral pH they need.
- **L233 · LISTIFY** (Absorption across the gut wall): “Fat needs special handling because it does not dissolve in water. Four terms describe how it is absorbed:”  
  *Problem:* A causal chain is first split into a four-term glossary and then retold step by step in the next paragraph, so the reader meets each step twice. The lead-in uses the 'N terms describe X:' pattern. Folding the definitions into the narrative removes the duplication.  
  *Draft:* Fat needs special handling because it does not dissolve in water. **Bile salts**, detergents that the liver makes from cholesterol, first emulsify it, breaking it into tiny droplets that lipase can reach. The products of digestion then cluster with bile salts into **mixed micelles**, which carry them to the brush border. There the fat diffuses into the enterocyte and the micelle stays behind: it acts as a shuttle and is not itself absorbed. Inside the cell the fat is re-esterified (reassembled into triglyceride; see the [*Macronutrients*](macronutrients.qmd) chapter) and packaged with cholesterol and protein into a **chylomicron**. Because a chylomicron is too big to enter a blood capillary, it passes instead into a **lacteal**, a lymph vessel, and reaches the blood through the lymph, bypassing the liver's first pass. This is why blood drawn after a fatty meal can look faintly milky.
- **L390 · JUMP** (Digestion and absorption in practice): “Viscous fibre has two effects: it slows digestion and it binds bile salts. Viscous fibres such as oat β-glucan and psyllium”  
  *Problem:* After the ultra-processed-food callout the section moves to fibre and then to fat ('Fat also aids...') with no bridge back to the table row 'Adding fat, protein, fibre or acid'. Both paragraphs are about what is absorbed rather than how fast, and the reader has to work that out. 'Viscous fibre... Viscous fibres' also repeats.  
  *Draft:* Two components of a meal also change what is absorbed, not only how fast. The first is viscous fibre, such as oat β-glucan and psyllium, which has two effects. It thickens the intestinal contents, slowing both gastric emptying and glucose absorption, and it binds bile salts, so that more are lost in stool. To replace them, the liver draws cholesterol from the blood, which is why adequate viscous fibre modestly lowers LDL cholesterol [@whitehead2014betaglucan]: in effect, the fibre interrupts the enterohepatic circulation (fibre fermentation is a separate process; see *The gut microbiome* chapter).  The second is fat, which aids the absorption of the fat-soluble vitamins. Vitamins A, D, E and K and the carotenoids ... [rest of paragraph unchanged]
